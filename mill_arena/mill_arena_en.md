# Karl Marx: Excerpts from James Mill's Book “Élémens d'économie politique”

*Trad. par J. T. Pariser, Paris 1823*[^1]

\[Written in the first half of 1844. From the manuscript. From Marx Engels Werke, vol. 40, Berlin 1985 {or Supplementary Volume, Part One, Berlin 1968}, pp. 443–463\]

|XXV| In that compensation between the value of money and the value of the metal, as in the presentation of the costs of production as the sole moment determining value, Mill—like the Ricardian school generally—makes the mistake of stating the *abstract law* without the change or continual abolition of that law through which alone it comes to be. If it is a *constant* law that, for example, the costs of production ultimately—or rather, when demand and supply sporadically and by chance[^2] coincide—determine price (value)[^3], it is just as *constant a law* that this relation fails to coincide, and therefore that value and costs of production stand in no necessary relation. Indeed, demand and supply coincide only momentarily, through their preceding fluctuation, through the disproportion between costs of production and exchange value, just as this fluctuation and disproportion in turn follow upon their momentary coincidence. This *actual* movement, of which that law is only an abstract, accidental, one-sided moment, is treated by modern political economy as an accident, as something inessential. Why? Because, given the precise and exact formulas to which it reduces political economy, its fundamental formula, were it to express that movement abstractly, would have to be: In political economy the law is determined by its opposite, lawlessness. The true law of political economy is *chance*; from its movement we, the scholars, arbitrarily fix certain moments in the form of laws. -

Mill puts the essence of the matter very well into a concept when he calls *money* the *mediator* of exchange. The essence of money is not, in the first place, that property is alienated in it, but that the *mediating activity* or movement, the *human*, social act by which human products mutually complement one another, becomes *estranged* and becomes the property of a *material thing* outside the human being: money. In alienating this mediating activity himself, the human being acts here only as one who has lost himself, as a dehumanized human being; the *relation* of the things (*Sachen*) themselves, the human activity involving them, becomes the activity of a being outside and above humanity. Through this *alien mediator*—whereas the human being himself ought to mediate for another human being—he sees his will, his activity and his relation to others as a power independent of them and of himself. His enslavement thus reaches its peak. That this *mediator* now becomes the *actual god* is clear: the mediator is the *actual power* over that with which it mediates me. Its worship becomes an end in itself. Objects separated from this mediator have lost their value. They have value only insofar as they *represent it*, whereas it originally appeared that it had value only insofar as *it represented them*. This reversal of the original relation is necessary. This *mediator* is therefore the self-lost, estranged *essence* of private property, private property become external to itself, *alienated* private property; it is likewise the *alienated mediation* of human production with human production, humanity's *alienated* species-activity. All the properties belonging to the human being in producing this activity are thus transferred to this mediator. The *richer* this mediator becomes, the poorer the human being becomes as a human being, that is, when separated from it. -

Christ originally *represents* 1. human beings before God; 2. God to human beings; 3. human beings to one another.

So, too, *money* originally represents, according to its concept: 1. private property to private property; 2. society to private property; 3. private property to society.

But Christ is the *alienated* God and the alienated *human being*. God has value only insofar as he represents Christ, and the human being has value only insofar as he represents Christ. The same holds for money. -

Why must private property develop into the *money-essence* (*Geldwesen*)? Because the human being, as a sociable being, must develop toward *exchange* ||XXV|[^4], and because exchange—on the presupposition of private property—must develop toward *value*. For the mediating movement of the exchanging human being is no social or human movement, no *human relation*: it is the *abstract relation* of private property to private property, and this *abstract* relation is *value*, whose actual existence as value is first attained in *money*. Because people exchanging things do not relate to one another as human beings, the *thing* (*Sache*) loses the significance of human, personal property. The social relation of private property to private property is already a relation in which private property is estranged from itself. Money, the independent existence of this relation, is therefore the alienation of private property, the abstraction from its *specific*, personal nature. —

The opposition of modern political economy to the monetary system, the Système monétaire, cannot therefore, despite all its cleverness, bring us any decisive victory. For if the crude political-economic superstition of the people and governments clings to the *sensuous, tangible, visible* purse and thus believes in the absolute value of the precious metals and in possessing them as the sole reality of wealth—if then the enlightened, worldly-wise political economist comes and proves to them that money is a commodity like any other, whose value, like that of any other commodity, depends on the relation of costs of production to demand competition[^5] and supply, to the quantity or competition of other commodities—he will rightly be told that the *actual* value of things is nevertheless their *exchange value*, which ultimately exists in money, just as money exists in precious metals; that money is therefore the *true* value of things and hence the most desirable thing. The economist's own teachings ultimately amount to just this wisdom, except that he has the capacity for abstraction needed to recognize this existence of money in every form of commodity and therefore does not believe in the exclusive value of its official metallic existence. The metallic existence of money is merely the official, perceptible expression of the spirit of money that inhabits every member of the productions and movements of bourgeois society. -

The modern political economists' opposition to the monetary system consists only in their having grasped the *money-essence* (*Geldwesen*) in its abstraction and universality, and thus seen through the *sensuous* superstition that believes this essence exists exclusively in precious metal. They replace this crude superstition with a refined one. But since both have the same essential root, the enlightened form of superstition cannot wholly displace its crude sensuous form: it attacks not the latter's essence but only one determinate form of that essence. The *personal* existence of money as money—not merely as the inner, implicit, hidden relation of intercourse or *relation of rank* among commodities—corresponds the more to the essence of money, the more abstract it is, the less *natural* its relation to other commodities, the more it appears both as a product and as a non-product of humanity, the less *naturally arisen* the element in which it exists and the more it is made by human beings; or, in the terms of political economy, the greater the *inverse* relation of its *value as money* to the exchange value or monetary value of the material in which it exists. Thus *paper money* and the multitude of *paper representatives of money* (bills of exchange, drafts, promissory notes, etc.) constitute a *more perfect* existence of *money as money*, and a necessary moment in the development of the money-essence. In the *credit system* (*Kreditwesen*), whose full expression is the *banking system* (*Bankwesen*), there arises the semblance that the power of the alien material power has been broken, that the relation of self-estrangement has been abolished, and that human beings have returned to human relations with one another. Deceived by this *semblance*, the *Saint-Simonists* regard the development of money, bills of exchange, paper money, paper representatives of money, *credit and the banking system* as a gradual abolition of the separation of the human being from the thing, of capital from labor, of private property from money and money from the human being, of one human being from another. The organized *banking system* is therefore their ideal. But this abolition of ||XXVI| estrangement, this *return* of human beings to themselves and hence to one another, is only a *semblance*: it is a self-estrangement and dehumanization all the more *infamous* and *extreme* because its element is no longer a commodity, metal or paper, but the *moral* existence, the *sociable* existence, the *inwardness* of the human breast itself; because beneath the semblance of human *trust* in humanity it is the utmost *distrust* and complete estrangement. What constitutes the essence of *credit*? Here we abstract entirely from the *content* of credit, which is once again money. Thus we abstract from the *content* of this trust, according to which one person *recognizes* another by advancing him values and—in the best case, when he does not charge for the credit, that is, when he is no usurer—by trusting his fellow human being to be no scoundrel but a “good” man.

By a “good” man the one who trusts means here, like Shylock, a “solvent” man. Credit is conceivable in two relations and under two different conditions. The two relations are: first, a rich person grants credit to a poor person whom he regards as industrious and orderly. This kind of credit belongs to the romantic, sentimental part of political economy, to its aberrations, excesses and *exceptions*, not to its *rule*. But even assuming this exception and granting this romantic possibility, the poor person's life, talents and activity count to the rich person as a *guarantee* of repayment of the loan: all the poor person's social virtues, the content of his life-activity, his very existence, represent to the rich person the repayment of his capital with the usual interest. The poor person's death is therefore the worst case for the lender: it is the death of his capital, interest included. Consider the baseness involved in *valuing* a person in *money*, as happens in the credit relation. Of course the lender has, in addition to *moral* guarantees, the guarantee of *legal* compulsion and more or less *material* guarantees for his man. If the borrower is himself wealthy, *credit* becomes no more than a *mediator* facilitating exchange: *money* itself is raised into a wholly *ideal* form. *Credit* is political economy's judgment of a person's *morality*. Under credit, instead of metal or paper, the *human being* himself has become the *mediator* of exchange—but not as a human being; rather as the *existence of capital* and interest. Thus the medium of exchange has indeed returned from its material shape to the human being, but only because the human being himself, displaced outside himself within himself, has become a material shape. It is not money that has been abolished in the human being within the credit relation; rather, the human being has been transformed into *money*, or money has been *incorporated* in him. *Human individuality* and human *morality* have become both commodities and the *material* in which money exists. Instead of money or paper, my own personal existence, my flesh and blood, my sociable virtue and standing are the matter, the body of the *money-spirit*. Credit now locates monetary value not in money, but in human flesh and in the human heart. Thus every advance and inconsistency within a false system is the deepest regression and the fullest consequence of baseness. Within the credit system its nature, estranged from human beings, manifests itself in two ways under the semblance of the highest recognition of human beings by political economy:

1\. The opposition between capitalist and worker, large and small capitalist, grows still greater, since credit is given only to those who already possess something, and gives the wealthy a new opportunity to accumulate; or else the poor person sees his *entire* existence affirmed or denied by the wealthy person's arbitrary whim and judgment, wholly dependent on this chance;

2\. mutual representation, hypocrisy and sanctimoniousness are driven so far that, beyond the simple judgment that he is poor, the person without credit now incurs the demoralizing judgment that he has no trust, no recognition, and is therefore a sociable pariah, a bad person; while the poor person suffers, in addition to his privation, this humiliation and the humiliating *request* for credit that he must make to the wealthy person; ||XXVII|

3\. because, through this wholly *ideal* existence of money, a person can counterfeit in no material other than his own person: he himself must become a false coin, obtain credit by stealth or lies, etc.; and this credit relation—on both the side of the trusting party and that of the person needing trust—becomes an object of trade, of mutual deceit and abuse. Here *distrust* brilliantly reveals itself as the basis of this political-economic trust: the suspicious weighing of whether credit should be granted; espionage into the secrets of the applicant's private life; disclosure of temporary difficulties in order to ruin a rival by suddenly shaking his credit, etc. The entire system of bankruptcy, sham undertakings, etc. … In *public credit* the state has exactly the same position that the person had above … The trading in government securities shows how the state has become the plaything of merchants, etc.

4\. Finally, the *credit system* finds its completion in the *banking system*. The creation of bankers, the bank's power over the state, the concentration of wealth in these hands—this political-economic *Areopagus* of the nation—is the fitting completion of the money-essence. As the *moral recognition of a human being*, like *trust in the state*, etc., takes the form of *credit* in the credit system, the secret hidden in the lie of moral recognition comes to light: the *immoral* baseness of this morality, together with the sanctimoniousness and egoism in that trust in the state; it reveals itself for what it actually is.

The *exchange* of human activity within production itself, as well as the exchange of *human products* with one another, is equivalent to *species-activity* and the spirit of the species, whose actual, conscious and true existence is *social* activity and *social* enjoyment. Since the *human* essence \[is\] the *true communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*) of human beings, human beings *create* and produce the human *communal being* by exercising their *essence*: the social essence, which is no abstract-universal power standing over against the single individual, but the essence of every individual, his own activity, his own life, his own spirit, his own wealth. This *true communal being* does not therefore arise from reflection; rather, it appears to be produced by individuals' *want* and *egoism*, that is, directly by the exercise of their existence itself. Whether this communal being exists does not depend upon human beings; but so long as the human being does not recognize himself as human and therefore has not organized the world humanly, this *communal being* appears in the form of *estrangement*. \\

For its *subject*, the human being, is a being estranged from himself. Human beings—not as abstractions but as actual, living, particular individuals—*are* this being. *As* they are, so is it. It is therefore one and the same proposition that the *human being* estranges himself, and that the *society* of this estranged human being is a caricature of his *actual communal being*, of his true species-life: consequently his activity appears as torment, his own creation as an alien power, his wealth as poverty, the *essential bond* connecting him to another human being as an inessential bond, and separation from another human being as his true existence; his life appears as the sacrifice of his life, the actualization of his essence as the de-actualization of his life, his production as the production of his nothingness, his power over the object as the object's power over him, and he, the lord of his creation, appears as its bondsman.

Political economy, then, conceives the *communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*) of *human beings*, or their actively expressed *human essence*, their mutual complementation into species-life, into truly human life, in the form of *exchange* and *trade*. *Society*, says Destutt de Tracy, is a *series of reciprocal échanges*.[^6] It is precisely this movement of mutual integration. *Society*, says Adam Smith, is a *trading society*. Each of its members is a *merchant*.[^7]

One sees how political economy *fixes* the *estranged* form of sociable intercourse as the *essential* and *original* form, the one corresponding to the human vocation.

||XXVIII| Political economy—like the actual movement—starts from the *relation of human being to human being* as the relation of *private proprietor to private proprietor*. If the human being is presupposed as a *private proprietor*, that is, an exclusive possessor who asserts his personality through this exclusive possession and thereby distinguishes himself from and relates to another human being—private property is his personal, his *distinguishing*, and hence his essential existence—then the *loss* or *relinquishment* of private property is an *alienation of the human being* as well as of *private property* itself. //

Here we retain only the latter determination. If I cede my private property to another, it ceases to be mine; it becomes a thing (*Sache*) independent of me, *outside* my sphere, a thing *external* to me. I thus *alienate* (*entäußere*) my private property. Relative to myself, I thus set it as *alienated* private property. But I set it only as an *alienated* thing in general: I abolish only my *personal* relation to it; I return it to the *elemental* forces of nature if I alienate it only relative to myself. It becomes *alienated private property* only if, at the same time, it ceases to be *my* private property without thereby ceasing to be *private property* at all—that is, if it enters into the same relation to *another* person *outside* me as it had to me, in short, if it becomes *another* person's *private property*. Leaving aside the case of *violence*, how do I come to alienate *my* private property to another person? Political economy rightly answers: from *want*, from *need*. The other person too is a private proprietor, but of a *different* thing which I lack and cannot or will not do without, a thing which appears to me as a *need* for the completion of my existence and the actualization of my essence.

The bond relating the two private proprietors to each other is the *specific nature of the object* that forms the matter of their private property. Longing for these two objects—that is, the need for them—shows each proprietor, brings to his consciousness, that besides private property he has another *essential* relation to objects: that he is not the particular being he takes himself to be, but a *total* being whose needs stand in a relation of *inner* property to all things, including the products of the other's labor. For the need for a thing is the clearest and most irrefutable proof that it belongs to *my* essence, that its being for me, its *property*, is the property, the peculiarity, of my essence. Thus both proprietors are driven to give up their private property, but to do so in a way that simultaneously confirms private property; they give up private property within the relation of private property. Each thus alienates a portion of his private property to the other.

The *social* connection or *social* relation between the two private proprietors is thus the *reciprocity* of *alienation*: the relation of alienation set on both sides, or *alienation* as the relation between the two proprietors; whereas in simple private property *alienation* still takes place only in relation to itself, on one side alone.

*Exchange* or *barter* is thus the social act, the species-act, the communal being (*Gemeinwesen*), the social intercourse and integration of human beings within *private property*, and therefore the external, *alienated* species-act. Precisely for this reason it appears as *barter*. It is thus equally the opposite of the *social* relation.

Through the reciprocal alienation or estrangement of private property, *private property* itself has acquired the determination of *alienated* private property. First, it has ceased to be the product of labor, the exclusive, distinguishing personality of its possessor: he has alienated it, it has passed away from the possessor whose product it was, and has acquired personal significance for someone whose product it *is not*. It has lost its personal significance for its possessor. Second, it has been brought into relation with another private property and set equal to it. A private property of *different* nature has taken its place, just as it itself stands in for a private property of *different* nature. Thus on both sides private property appears as the representative of a private property of another nature, as the *equal* of a *different* natural product; the two sides relate to each other so that each represents the existence of its *other*, each acting reciprocally as a *substitute* for itself and the other. The existence of private property as such has thus become a *substitute*, an *equivalent*. Instead of its immediate unity with itself, it now exists only in relation to an *other*. As an *equivalent*, its existence is no longer peculiar to itself. It has thus become *value*, and directly *exchange value*. Its existence as *value* is a determination ||XXIX| of *itself* different from its immediate existence, external to its specific essence, *alienated*: a merely *relative* existence.

How this *value* is determined more precisely, and how it becomes price, must be developed elsewhere.

Given the relation of exchange, *labor* becomes *immediate labor-for-gain*. This relation of estranged labor reaches its height only when 1. on the one hand *labor-for-gain*, the worker's product, stands in no *immediate* relation to his need or his *vocation for labor*, but is determined in both respects by social combinations alien to the worker; 2. the person who *accumulates* the product does not himself produce, but exchanges what someone else produced. In that crude form of *alienated* private property, *barter*, each of the two private proprietors produced what his need, his disposition and the natural material at hand directly impelled him to produce. Each therefore exchanges with the other only the surplus of his production. Labor was indeed his immediate *source of subsistence*, but also the exercise of his *individual existence*. Through exchange his *labor* has become in part a *source of gain*. Its purpose and its existence have diverged. The product is produced as *value*, as *exchange value*, as *equivalent*, no longer for the sake of its immediate personal relation to the producer. The more varied production becomes, the more varied needs become on the one hand and the more one-sided the producer's accomplishments become on the other, the more his labor falls into the category of *labor-for-gain*, until that is its sole significance. Whether the producer then stands in a relation of immediate enjoyment and personal need to his product, and whether the *activity*, the action of labor itself, is for him an enjoyment of his own personality and the actualization of his natural capacities and intellectual purposes, become entirely *accidental* and *inessential*.

*Labor-for-gain* entails:

1\. the estrangement and contingency of labor relative to the laboring subject;

2\. the estrangement and contingency of labor relative to its object;

3\. the determination of the worker by social needs, which are alien to him and a compulsion to which he submits out of egoistic need, out of want; they mean to him only a source for meeting his necessities, just as he exists for them only as a slave to their needs;

4\. that preserving his individual existence appears to the worker as the *end* of his activity, while his actual doing figures for him only as a means: he exercises his life in order to obtain means of life.

Thus the greater and more developed the social power appears within the relation of private property, the more *egoistic*, bereft of society and estranged from his own essence the human being becomes.

Just as the reciprocal exchange of the products of *human activity* appears as *barter*, as *huckstering*, the mutual complementation and exchange of activity itself appears as the *division of labor*, which turns the human being as far as possible into an abstract being, a lathe, etc., and transforms him into an intellectual and physical monstrosity.

Precisely the *unity* of human labor is seen only as *division*, because the social essence comes into existence only as its opposite, in the form of estrangement. The *division of labor* increases with civilization.

Given the division of labor, the product, the material of private property, increasingly takes on the meaning of an *equivalent* for the individual. Since he no longer exchanges his *surplus*, and the object he produces may be altogether *indifferent* to him, he no longer exchanges his product directly for the being *in need* of it. The equivalent acquires its existence as equivalent in *money*, which is now the immediate result of labor-for-gain and the *mediator* of exchange. (See above.)

In *money*, in its complete indifference to the nature of the material, the specific nature of private property and the personality of the private proprietor, the complete dominion of the estranged thing (*Sache*) *over* the human being has become apparent. What had been the dominion of person over person is now the universal dominion of the *thing* over the *person*, of product over producer. Just as the determination of the *alienation* of private property already lay in the *equivalent*, in value, so *money* is the sensuous, itself objective existence of this *alienation*.

||XXX| It goes without saying that political economy can conceive this whole development only as a fact, as the offspring of accidental want.

The separation of labor from itself = the separation of the worker from the capitalist = the separation of labor and capital, whose original form divides into *landed property* and *movable[^8] property* … The original determination of private property is monopoly; as soon as it gives itself a political constitution, that constitution is monopoly. The completed monopoly is competition. To the political economist *production* and *consumption* fall apart, along with their mediator, *exchange* or *distribution*. The separation of production and consumption, of activity and spirit[^9], among different individuals and within the same individual is the *separation of labor* from its *object* and from itself as spirit. *Distribution* is the power of private property in action. The mutual separation of labor, capital and landed property, as well as of labor from labor, capital from capital and landed property from landed property; then the separation of labor from wages, capital from profit and profit from interest; and finally of landed property from ground rent—these make self-estrangement appear both in the form of self-estrangement and in that of reciprocal estrangement.

“Suppose now that the government wants to fix the increase and decrease of money. If it tries to keep the quantity of money below the level that the free course of things would produce, it raises the value of coined gold and gives everyone an interest in turning bullion into coin. Clandestine minting then arises, and the government must prevent it by punishment. If the government seeks to maintain the quantity of gold *above* the required level, it lowers its value, and everyone seeks to melt it down into bullion; again, punishment is the only recourse. But the hope of profit prevails over the fear of punishment.” pp. 137–138.

§ 9. “If two individuals each owed the other £100, instead of paying one another that sum they need only exchange their obligations. The same holds between two nations … Hence *bills of exchange*, all the more necessary at a time when unenlightened policy prohibited and cruelly punished the *export* of precious metals.” p. 142 ff.

§ 10. Saving *unproductive* consumption through paper money, p. 146 ff.

§ 11. “The disadvantages of paper money are: 1. the *failure*” sin[^10] (*manque*) “of those who issue paper money to fulfil their obligations. 2. Counterfeits. 3. Changes in its rate of exchange, *altération des cours*.” p. 149.

§ 12. Precious metals are commodities. Commodities are exported only when they are cheaper in the country from which they come than in the country to which they go, and imported only when they are dearer in the country to which they go than in the one from which they come. Thus whether \[one\] should import or export precious metals depends on their value in a country. \[p. 175 ff.\]

§ 13. “The value of the precious metals is the quantity of other things given in exchange for them.” p. 177. “This relation differs between countries and even between different parts of the same country. … *‘La vie est moins chère’*[^11] means that at a particular place one can buy[^12] means of life for a smaller sum of money.” \[p. 177.\]

§ 14. “The relation between nations is like that between merchants … ils achéteront toujours au meilleur marché possible, et vendront toujours le plus cher qu'ils pourront[^13].” p. 215.

IV\. *De la consommation.[^14]*

“*Production, distribution, échange*[^15] are mere *means*. One does not produce for the sake of producing. They are intermediate, mediating operations. The end is *consumption*.” p. 237.

§ 1. “Consumption is: 1. *productive*. It includes everything spent in order to produce a thing: the worker's subsistence … then the machines, tools, buildings and animals used for productive operations; finally, raw materials, whether the object to be produced is formed directly from them or can be obtained from them.” pp. 238–239. “Only the things of the second class are not completely consumed in the course of productive operations.” loc. cit.

2\. *unproductive* consumption. “A lackey's wages: every consumption not undertaken for the sake of products, in order to obtain by means of one thing another equivalent to it, is unproductive.” p. 240. “Productive consumption is itself a *means*, namely a means of production; unproductive consumption is no means but an end: the *enjoyment* supplied by consumption, the *motive* of all the preceding operations.” p. 241. “Through the first kind of consumption nothing is lost; through the second everything is lost.” loc. cit. “What is *productively* consumed is always *capital*. This is a particularly remarkable property of productive consumption. What is productively consumed *is* capital, and it *becomes* capital through consumption.” pp. 241–242. “The totality of what a country's productive forces create in one year is the *produit annuel brut*[^16]. The greater part of it is destined to replace the capital consumed. What remains of the *produit brut*[^17] after capital has been replaced is the *produit net*[^18]; it is distributed only as profit on capital or ground rent.” pp. 242–243. “This is the fund from which all additions to the nation's capital ordinarily come.” loc. cit. “*Productive* and *unproductive labor* correspond to *productive* and *unproductive consumption*.” p. 244.

§ 2. “Everything produced during one year is consumed during the following year, productively or unproductively.” p. 246.

§ 3. “Consumption expands in proportion to production: a person produces only because he wants *to have*. If the thing produced is what he wants, he stops working once he has obtained as much as he needs. If he produces more, it is because he wants to have some other object in exchange for the excess. He produces one thing out of a desire to possess another. Producing the one is his sole means of obtaining the other, and he obtains it more cheaply than if he were obliged to produce it himself. With the division of labor he confines himself to producing a particular thing or even just a part of it, using only a small portion of his own production for himself; the rest he assigns to the purchase of all the other commodities he wants. If a person confines himself to producing just one thing and exchanges his product for all the others, we find that everyone receives more of the various things he desires than he would have received had he wished to produce them ||XXXI| himself. If someone produces for himself, *exchange* does not occur. He seeks to buy nothing and offers nothing for sale. He possesses an object, has produced it, and does not intend to part with it. If the terms ‘offre und demande’[^19] are applied metaphorically to this case, supply and demand are here fully proportioned. Concerning the supply and demand of saleable objects, we may leave out of account entirely that portion of the annual product which each *producteur* consumes in the form in which he produces or receives it.” p. 251.

“When we speak here of *offre* and *demande*, we speak in general. When we say of a given nation at a given time that its supply equals its demand, we do not mean this with regard to one or two commodities; we mean that its demand for all commodities taken *en masse* equals everything it can offer in commodities of every kind. Despite this equality of supply and demand in general, one or more particular commodities may very well have been produced in too great or too small a quantity relative to demand for them.” pp. 251–252. “Two things are needed to constitute a *demand* (*demande*): the desire for a commodity and possession of an equivalent object that can be given in exchange. A *demande* means both the *desire* and the *means* to buy. If either is lacking, no purchase can take place. Possession of an equivalent object is the necessary basis of every demand. It is in vain that a person wishes to possess things if he has nothing to give in order to obtain them. The equivalent object a person brings is the *instrument* of demand. The extent of his demand is measured by the value of that object. Demand and the equivalent object are terms that can be substituted *for one another*. We have already seen that every person who produces strives to possess things other than the one in whose production he has taken part, and that the extent of this striving, of this desire, is *measured* by the totality of his production insofar as he does not wish to retain it for his own consumption. Equally clearly, a person can offer in exchange for other objects whatever he has produced and does not wish to consume himself. His *will to buy* and his *means of doing so* are therefore equal; or his demand exactly equals his total product insofar as he does not wish to consume it himself.” pp. 252–253.

Here Mill analyzes exchange on the basis of private property with his customary cynical acuity and clarity.

The human being—this is the fundamental presupposition of private property—*produces* only in order *to have*. The purpose of production is *having*. And production has not only this *useful* purpose: it has a *self-interested* purpose. The human being produces only in order *to have* for himself; the object of his production is the objectification of his *immediate*, self-interested *need*. For himself, then—in the savage, barbarous state—the human being has the measure of his production in the *extent* of his immediate need, whose content is *immediately* the produced object itself.

In this state, therefore, the human being produces *no more* than he immediately needs. The *limit of his need* is the *limit of his production*. Demand and supply therefore coincide exactly. His production is *measured* by his need. In this case there is no exchange, or exchange is reduced to the exchange of his labor for its product; this exchange is the latent form, the germ[^20], of actual exchange.

As soon as exchange takes place, surplus production beyond the immediate limit of possession takes place. This surplus production is not, however, a transcendence of self-interested need. It is rather merely a *mediated* way of satisfying a need whose objectification is found not directly in this production but in another person's production. Production has become a *source of gain*, labor-for-gain. Whereas in the first relation need is the measure of production, in the second, production—or rather *possession of the product*—is the measure of the extent to which needs can be met.

I have produced for myself, not for you, just as you have produced for yourself, not for me. The result of my production has in and of itself as little relation to you as the result of yours has any immediate relation to me. That is, our production is[^21] not a production of human beings for human beings *as such*, not a *social* production. Thus neither of us, as a human being, has a relation of enjoyment to the other's product. As human beings we do not exist for each other's productions. Our exchange therefore cannot be the mediating movement confirming that my product ||XXXII| is \[for\] you because it is an *objectification* of your own essence, your need. For the *human essence* is not the bond connecting our productions to one another. Exchange can only set in *motion* and confirm the *character* each of us bears toward his own product, and thus toward the other's production. Each sees in his own product only his *own* objectified self-interest, and thus in the other's product an *other*, independent, alien, objective self-interest.

Certainly, as a human being you have a human relation to my product: you have a *need* for it. So it exists for you as an object of desire and will. But your need, desire and will are powerless need, desire and will with respect to my product. That is, your *human* essence, which necessarily stands in an inner relation to my human production, is not your *power*, your property in that production, because the *peculiarity*, the *power* of the human essence is not recognized in my production. Rather, these are the *bond* that makes you dependent on me, because they place you in dependence on my product. Far from being the *means* of giving you *power* over my production, they are the *means* of giving me power over you.

If I produce *more* of an object than I can immediately use myself, my surplus production is *calculated* for your need, artfully contrived. It is only in *semblance* that I produce more of that object. In truth I produce an *other* object, the object you produce, for which I intend to exchange my surplus—an exchange I have already accomplished in thought. The *social* relation between us, my labor for your need, is therefore merely a *semblance*, and our mutual complementation is likewise a mere *semblance* founded on mutual plunder. The intention to *plunder* and *defraud* necessarily lies in wait: since our exchange is self-interested on your side and on mine, and since each interest seeks to outdo the other, we necessarily seek to deceive each other. To become an actual power, the degree of power I grant my object over yours does, of course, require your *recognition*. But our mutual recognition of the relative power of our objects is a struggle, and in a struggle the one with more energy, force, insight or skill prevails. If physical force is enough, I rob you directly. Once the realm of physical force has been broken, we each try to create an appearance for the other, and the more skilful takes advantage of the other. Which one gets the better of the other is a matter of chance for the *whole* relation. The *ideal, intended* advantage-taking occurs on both sides: each of us judges himself to have got the better of the other.

Exchange is therefore necessarily mediated on both sides by the *object* of mutual production and mutual possession. The ideal relation to the respective objects of our production is indeed our mutual need. But the *real* relation, the one set in *actuality*, the *true* relation that is carried out, is only mutual *exclusive possession* of what each produces. Only your *object*, the *equivalent* of mine, gives your need for my thing (*Sache*) any *value*, *dignity* or *effect* for me. Our respective products are thus the *means*, the *mediation*, the *instrument*, the *recognized power* of our respective needs over one another. Your *demand* and the *equivalent in your possession* are thus terms with the *same meaning* and the same validity for me; your demand has meaning, and thus an effect, only insofar as it has meaning and effect in relation to me. As a mere human being, without this instrument, your demand is an unsatisfied aspiration on your part, a nonexistent idea for me. As a human being you therefore stand in no relation to my object, because *I myself* have no human relation to it. But the *means* is the *true power* over an object, and so we each see our product as the *power* of each over the other and over himself. In other words, our own product has reared up against us; it seemed to be our property, but in truth we are its property. We ourselves are excluded from *true* property, because our *property* excludes the other human being.

The only intelligible language we speak to one another is that of our objects in their relation to each other. We would not understand a human language; it would have no effect. On one side it would be known and felt as a request, a plea ||XXXIII| and thus as a *humiliation*, and uttered with shame and a feeling of being cast aside; on the other it would be received and rejected as *impertinence* or *madness*. So estranged are we from each other's human essence that the direct language of that essence appears to us as a *violation of human dignity*, while the estranged language of thingly (*sachlich*) values appears as rightful, self-confident human dignity that recognizes itself.

To be sure, in your eyes your product is an *instrument*, a *means* for taking possession of my product and thus meeting your need. But in my eyes it is the *end* of our exchange. You figure for me rather as a means and instrument for producing this object, which is my end, just as in this relation I figure for you in relation to my object. But 1. each of us really *does* what the other sees him as doing. You have really made yourself a means, an instrument, the producer of *your own* object, in order to take possession of mine. 2. Your own object is for you only the *sensuous covering*, the *hidden shape* of my object, for its production *means*, seeks to *express*, the *acquisition* of my object. Thus you have in fact become, for yourself, a *means*, an *instrument* of your object, whose *bondsman* your desire is; you have rendered bondsman's service so that the object of your desire might never again do you a favor. If, at the beginning of this development, this mutual bondage under the object does in fact appear as a relation of *lordship* and *slavery*, that is merely the *crude* and candid expression of our *essential* relation.

Our *mutual* worth is for us the *value* of our respective objects. Thus the human being himself is mutually *worthless* to us.

Suppose we had produced as human beings: each of us would have *doubly affirmed* himself and the other in his production. I would have, 1. in my *production*, objectified my *individuality*, its *peculiarity*, and thus enjoyed both an individual *manifestation of life (Lebensäußerung)* in the course of the activity and, in contemplating the object, the individual joy of knowing my personality as an *objective, sensuously perceptible* power and hence *beyond all doubt*. 2. In your enjoyment or use of my product I would have *immediately* enjoyed both the consciousness of having met a *human* need through my labor, thus of having objectified the *human* essence and provided an object corresponding to the need of another *human* being; 3. of having been the *mediator* for you between yourself and the species, and so of being known and felt by you as a complement to your own essence and a necessary part of yourself, thus of knowing myself confirmed in your thought and in your love; 4. of having directly created your manifestation of life through my individual manifestation of life, and so directly confirmed and actualized through my individual activity my true essence, my *human essence*, my *communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*).

Our productions would be so many mirrors from which our essence would shine back at us.

This relation would be reciprocal: let what happens on my side happen on yours.

Let us consider the different moments as they appear in this supposition:

My labor would be *free manifestation of life (Lebensäußerung)*, and hence *enjoyment* of *life*. Under the presupposition of private property it is *alienation of life (Lebensentäußrung)*, for I work in order *to live*, in order to obtain a *means* of life. My working *is not* life.

Second: in labor there would therefore be the *peculiarity* of my individuality, since my *individual* life would be affirmed. Labor would therefore be *true, active property*. Under the presupposition of private property my individuality is alienated to such a degree that this *activity* is *hateful* to me, a *torment*; rather, it is only the *semblance* of an activity, and thus only a *forced* activity, imposed on me by an *external*, accidental want, *not* by an *inner, necessary* want.

My labor can appear in my object only as what it is. It cannot appear as what it essentially *is not*. Hence it appears only as the objective, sensuous, contemplated expression—beyond all doubt—of my *self-loss* and my *impotence*. |XXXIII||[^22]
