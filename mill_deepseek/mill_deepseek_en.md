# Karl Marx: Excerpts from James Mill's Book “Élémens d'économie politique”

*Trad. par J. T. Pariser, Paris 1823*[^1]

\[Written in the first half of 1844. From the manuscript. According to Marx Engels Werke, Volume 40, Berlin 1985 {or Supplementary Volume, Part One, Berlin 1968}, pp. 443-463\]

|XXV| In that compensation of money and metal value, as in the presentation of production costs as the sole moment in the determination of value, Mill – like the school of Ricardo in general – commits the error of expressing the *abstract law* without the change or the constant abolition of this law – through which it first comes to be. If it is a *constant* law that, e.g., production costs in the last instance – or rather in the sporadically accidental[^2] coincidence of demand and supply – determine the price (value)[^3], then it is an equally *constant* law that this relation does not coincide, hence that value and production costs stand in no necessary relation. Indeed, demand and supply coincide only momentarily, through the preceding fluctuation of demand and supply, through the disproportion between production costs and exchange value, just as this fluctuation and this disproportion in turn follow upon the momentary coincidence. This *actual* movement, of which that law is only an abstract, accidental and one-sided moment, is made into an accident, into something inessential, by modern political economy. Why? Because with the sharp and exact formulas to which they reduce political economy, the fundamental formula, if they wanted to express that movement abstractly, would have to read: The law in political economy is determined by its opposite, lawlessness. The true law of political economy is *chance*, from whose movement we, the scientists, arbitrarily fix a few moments in the form of laws. -

It is very good, and brings the essence of the matter into a concept, when Mill designates *money* as the *mediator* of exchange. The essence of money is not, in the first place, that property is alienated in it, but that the *mediating activity* or movement, the *human*, social act through which the products of man mutually complement one another, becomes *estranged* and acquires the character of a *material thing* outside man, of money. In that man himself alienates this mediating activity, he is active here only as a man who has lost himself, a dehumanized man; the *relation* of the things themselves, the human operation with them, becomes the operation of a being outside man and above man. Through this *alien mediator* – instead of man himself being the mediator for man – man beholds his will, his activity, his relation to others as a power independent of him and of them. His slavery thus reaches its peak. That this *mediator* now becomes the *actual God* is clear, for the mediator is the *actual power* over that with which it mediates me. Its cult becomes an end in itself. The objects, separated from this mediator, have lost their value. Thus only insofar as they *represent it* do they have value, whereas originally it seemed that it had value only insofar as *it* represented *them*. This inversion of the original relation is necessary. This *mediator* is therefore the self-lost, estranged *essence* of private property, the private property that has become external to itself, *alienated* (*entäußerte*) private property, just as it is the *alienated* mediation of human production with human production, the *alienated* species-activity of man. All the properties that belong to the latter in the production of this activity are therefore transferred to this mediator. Man thus becomes the poorer as man, i.e. separated from this mediator, the *richer* this mediator becomes. -

Christ originally *represents*: 1. men before God; 2. God for men; 3. men to men.

Thus *money*, in its concept, originally represents: 1. private property for private property; 2. society for private property; 3. private property for society.

But Christ is the *alienated* God and the alienated *man*. God has value only insofar as he represents Christ, man only insofar as he represents Christ. The same with money. -

Why must private property proceed to *money-essence* (*Geldwesen*)? Because man, as a *sociable being*, must proceed to *exchange* ||XXV|[^4], and because exchange – under the presupposition of private property – must proceed to *value*. The mediating movement of the exchanging man is namely not a social, not a human movement, not a *human relation*; it is the *abstract relation* of private property to private property, and this *abstract* relation is *value*, whose actual existence as value is first *money*. Because the exchanging men do not relate to one another as men, the thing (*Sache*) loses the significance of human, personal property. The social relation of private property to private property is already a relation in which private property is estranged from itself. The self-subsistent existence of this relation, money, is therefore the alienation of private property, the abstraction from its *specific*, personal nature. —

The opposition of modern political economy to the monetary system, *Système monétaire*, can therefore, despite all its cleverness, bring us no decisive victory; for if the crude political-economic superstition of the people and of governments clings to the *sensuous, tangible, conspicuous* money-bag, and therefore believes in the absolute value of the noble metals and in their possession as the sole reality of wealth – if then the enlightened, worldly-wise political economist comes and proves to them that money is a commodity like any other, whose value therefore, like that of every other commodity, depends on the relation of production costs to demand, competition[^5] and supply, to the quantity or competition of the other commodities – then it is rightly replied to this political economist that the *actual* value of things is nevertheless their *exchange value*, and that this exists in the last instance in money, as money exists in the noble metals, so that money is the *true* value of things and therefore the most desirable thing. For the teachings of the political economist themselves ultimately come down to this wisdom, except that he possesses the capacity for abstraction to recognize this existence of money under all forms of commodities, and therefore does not believe in the exclusive value of its official metallic existence. – The metallic existence of money is only the official, perceptible expression of the money-soul that resides in all the members of the productions and movements of bourgeois society. -

The opposition of the modern political economists to the monetary system is only this, that they have grasped the *money-essence* (*Geldwesen*) in its abstraction and universality, and are therefore enlightened about the *sensuous* superstition that believes in the exclusive existence of this essence in the noble metal. They put the refined superstition in the place of this crude one. But because both have one root in the essence, the enlightened form of superstition does not succeed in completely displacing the crude sensuous form of it, because it attacks not its essence but only the determinate form of this essence. – The *personal* existence of money as money – and not merely as the inner, self-subsistent, hidden intercourse-relation or *status-relation* of commodities to one another – this existence corresponds all the more to the essence of money, the more abstract it is, the less *natural* relation it has to the other commodities, the more it appears as a product and yet again as a non-product of man, the less *naturally grown* its element of existence, the more it is created by man, or, in political-economic terms, the greater the *inverse* relation of its *value as money* to the exchange value or money value of the material in which it exists. Hence *paper money* and the number of *paper representatives of money* (such as bills of exchange, mandates, promissory notes, etc.) are the *more perfect* existence of *money as money* and a necessary moment in the progress of the development of the money-essence. In the *credit system* (*Kreditwesen*), whose complete expression is the *banking system* (*Bankwesen*), it acquires the semblance that the power of the alien material power has been broken, the relation of self-estrangement abolished, and man restored to human relations with man. The *Saint-Simonists*, deceived by this *semblance*, regard the development of money, bills of exchange, paper money, paper representatives of money, *credit, banking system* as a gradual abolition of the separation of man from the thing, of capital from labor, of private property from money and of money from man, of the separation of man from man. The organized *banking system* is therefore their ideal. But this abolition of ||XXVI| estrangement, this *return* of man to himself and therefore to the other man, is only a *semblance*; it is a self-estrangement, a dehumanization all the more *infamous* and *extreme* because its element is no longer commodity, metal, paper, but the *moral* existence, the *sociable* existence, the *inner* being of the human breast itself; because, under the semblance of man's *trust* in man, it is the highest *distrust* and complete estrangement. What constitutes the essence of *credit*? We here abstract entirely from the *content* of credit, which is again money. We therefore abstract from the *content* of this trust, according to which one man *recognizes* another by advancing him values and – in the best case, namely when he does not have the credit repaid, i.e. is no usurer – bestows on his fellow man the trust that he is not a rogue but a “good” man.

By a “good” man the trusting party here understands, like Shylock, a “solvent” man. – Credit is conceivable under 2 relations and under 2 different conditions. The 2 relations are: first, a rich man gives credit to a poor man whom he regards as industrious and orderly. This kind of credit belongs to the romantic, sentimental part of political economy, to its aberrations, excesses, *exceptions*, not to the *rule*. But even supposing this exception, granting this romantic possibility, the life of the poor man and his talent as well as activity figure for the rich man as a *guarantee* of the repayment of the money lent; that is to say, all the social virtues of the poor man, the content of his life-activity, his very existence, represent for the rich man the reimbursement of his capital with the usual interest. The death of the poor man is therefore the worst case for the creditor. It is the death of his capital together with the interest. Consider what baseness lies in the *valuation* of a man in *money*, as it occurs in the credit relation. It goes without saying that the creditor, besides the *moral* guarantees, also has the guarantee of *juridical* coercion and, more or less, *real* guarantees for his man. If now the person to whom credit is given is himself wealthy, then *credit* becomes merely a facilitating *mediator* of exchange, i.e. *money* itself is raised into a wholly *ideal* form. *Credit* is the *political-economic* judgment about a man's *morality*. In credit, instead of metal or paper, the *man* himself has become the *mediator* of exchange, but not as man, rather as the *existence of a capital* and of interest. The medium of exchange has thus indeed returned from its material shape into man and been transposed back, but only because man himself, transposed outside himself within himself, has become a material shape. It is not that money is abolished in man – within the credit relation – but man himself is transformed into *money*, or money is *incorporated* in him. *Human individuality*, human *morality*, has itself become both an article of trade and the *material* in which money exists. Instead of money, paper, my own personal existence, my flesh and blood, my sociable virtue and standing, is the matter, the body of the *money-spirit*. Credit no longer separates money value into money, but into human flesh and human heart. To such an extent are all advances and inconsistencies within a false system the highest regression and the highest consequence of baseness. – Within the credit system its nature estranged from man is active in a twofold way under the semblance of the highest political-economic recognition of man:

1\. The opposition between capitalist and worker, large and small capitalist, becomes even greater, in that credit is given only to him who already has, and is a new chance of accumulation for the rich; or in that the poor man sees his *entire* existence confirmed or denied in the accidental whim and judgment of the rich man about him, sees it wholly dependent on this accident;

2\. in that mutual representation, hypocrisy and sanctimoniousness are driven to the peak, so that to the simple judgment that he is poor there is now added, for the man without credit, the demoralizing judgment that he possesses no trust, no recognition, and is therefore a sociable pariah, a bad man; and in that the poor man, in addition to his privation, receives this humiliation and the humiliating *entreaty* for credit from the rich man; ||XXVII|

3\. in that, through this wholly *ideal* existence of money, counterfeiting can be carried out by man on no other material but only on his own person; he himself must make himself into false coin, obtain credit by stealth, lie for it, etc., and this credit relation – both on the side of the trusting party and on that of the one who needs the trust – becomes an object of trade, an object of mutual fraud and abuse. Here *distrust* then reveals itself brilliantly as the basis of this political-economic trust; the distrustful weighing of whether credit should be granted or not; the espionage into the secrets of the private life, etc., of the applicant for credit; the betrayal of momentary difficulties in order to overthrow a rival by suddenly shaking his credit, etc. The whole system of bankruptcy, the sham enterprises, etc. … In *state credit* the state has exactly the same position that man had above … In the play with state papers it shows how it has become a plaything of the merchants, etc.

4\. The *credit system* finally has its completion in the *banking system*. The creation of the bankers, the state dominion of the bank, the concentration of wealth in these hands, this political-economic *Areopagus* of the nation, is the worthy completion of the money-essence. In that in the credit system the *moral recognition of a man*, like *trust in the state*, etc., received the form of *credit*, there emerges the secret that lies in the lie of moral recognition, the *immoral* baseness of this morality, as well as the sanctimoniousness and egoism in that trust in the state, and it shows itself as what it actually is.

The *exchange* both of human activity within production itself and of *human products* with one another is = the *species-activity* and species-spirit, whose actual, conscious and true existence is *social* activity and *social* enjoyment. Since the *human* essence is the *true communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*) of men [is], men *create*, produce through the exercise of their *essence* the human *communal being*, the social essence, which is not an abstract-universal power over the single individual, but is the essence of each individual, his own activity, his own life, his own spirit, his own wealth. It is therefore not through reflection that this *true communal being* arises; it therefore appears produced through the *want* and the *egoism* of individuals, i.e. immediately through the exercise of their existence itself. It does not depend on man whether this communal being is or is not; but as long as man does not recognize himself as man and therefore has not organized the world humanly, this *communal being* appears under the form of *estrangement*. \\

Because its *subject*, man, is a being estranged from itself. Men, not in an abstraction but as actual, living, particular individuals, *are* this being. *As* they are, so is it itself. It is therefore an identical proposition that *man* estranges himself, and that the *society* of this estranged man is the caricature of his *actual communal being*, of his true species-life; that therefore his activity appears as torment, his own creation as an alien power, his wealth as poverty, the *essential bond* that ties him to the other man as an inessential bond, and rather the separation from the other man as his true existence; that his life appears as the sacrifice of his life, that the actualization of his essence appears as the de-actualization of his life, that his production appears as the production of his nothingness, that his power over the object appears as the power of the object over him, that he, the lord of his creation, appears as the bondsman of this creation.

Now political economy comprehends the *communal being* of *man*, or their self-exercising *human essence*, their reciprocal complementation into species-life, into truly human life, under the form of *exchange* and *trade*. *Society*, says Destutt de Tracy, is a *series* *of mutual échanges.*[^6] It is precisely this movement of mutual integration. *Society*, says Adam Smith, is a *trading society*. Each of its members is a *merchant*.[^7]

One sees how political economy *fixes* the *estranged* form of sociable intercourse as the *essential* and *original* form, and as corresponding to the human determination.

||XXVIII| Political economy – like the actual movement – proceeds from the *relation of man to man* as that of *private proprietor to private proprietor*. If man is presupposed as *private proprietor*, i.e. as exclusive possessor, who proves his personality through this exclusive possession and distinguishes himself from the other man, and relates to him – private property is his personal, his *distinguishing*, therefore his essential existence – then the *loss* or the *giving up* of private property is an *alienation of man*, as of *private property* itself. //

We hold fast here only to the latter determination. If I relinquish my private property to another, it ceases to be mine; it becomes a thing (*Sache*) independent of me, lying *outside* my sphere, a thing *external* to me. I thus alienate (*entäußere*) my private property. In relation to myself, I therefore set it as *alienated* private property. But I set it only as an *alienated thing* in general; I abolish only my *personal* relation to it; I give it back to the *elemental* powers of nature, if I alienate it only in relation to myself. It becomes *alienated private property* only when it at the same time ceases to be *my* private property, without thereby ceasing to be *private property* in general, i.e. when it enters into the same relation to *another* man *outside* me in which it stood to me myself; in a word, when it becomes the *private property* of *another* man. Leaving aside the case of *violence* – how then do I come to alienate *my* private property to another man? Political economy answers correctly: out of *want*, out of *need*. The other man is also a private proprietor, but of a *different* thing, which I lack and which I cannot or will not do without, which seems to me a *need* for the completion of my existence and the actualization of my essence.

The bond that relates the two private proprietors to one another is the *specific nature of the object* that is the matter of their private property. The longing for these two objects, i.e. the need for them, shows each of the private proprietors, brings it to his consciousness, that apart from private property he still has another *essential* relation to the objects, that he is not the particular being he takes himself to be, but a *total* being, whose needs stand, in the relation of *inner* property, to all things, including the productions of the other's labor – for the need for a thing is the most evident, most irrefutable proof that the thing belongs to *my* essence, that its being for me, its *property*, is the property, the peculiarity of my essence. Both proprietors are therefore driven to give up their private property, but to give it up in such a way that they at the same time confirm private property, or give up private property within the relation of private property. Each therefore alienates a part of his private property to the other.

The *social* relation or the *social* relationship of the two private proprietors is therefore the *reciprocity* of *alienation*, the relation of alienation posited on both sides, or *alienation* as the relation of the two proprietors, whereas in simple private property *alienation* still takes place only in relation to itself, one-sidedly.

*Exchange* or *barter* is therefore the social, the species-act, the communal being, the social intercourse and integration of men within *private property*, and therefore the external, the *alienated* species-act. Precisely for this reason it appears as *barter*. It is therefore equally the opposite of the *social* relation.

Through the reciprocal alienation or estrangement of private property, *private property* itself has come into the determination of *alienated* private property. For firstly, it has ceased to be the product of labor, the exclusive, distinguishing personality of its possessor, for he has alienated it; it has departed from the possessor whose product it was, and has acquired a personal significance for the one whose product it *is not*. It has lost its personal significance for the possessor. Secondly, it has been related to another private property, has been set equal to it. In its place a private property of *other* nature has stepped in, just as it itself takes the place of a private property of *other* nature. On both sides, therefore, private property appears as the representative of a private property of another nature, as the *equal* of an *other* natural product, and both sides relate to one another in such a way that each represents the existence of its *other*, and both reciprocally relate to one another as *substitutes* of themselves and of their other. The existence of private property as such has therefore become a *substitute*, an *equivalent*. In place of its immediate unity with itself, it is now only as a relation to an *other*. As *equivalent*, its existence is no longer its own peculiar existence. It has therefore become *value* and immediately *exchange value*. Its existence as *value* is a determination of *itself* different from its immediate existence, external to its specific essence, an *alienated* determination ||XXIX| of itself, a merely *relative* existence of it.

How this *value* is further determined is to be developed elsewhere, as also how it becomes price.

With the relation of exchange presupposed, *labor* becomes *immediate labor-for-gain*. This relation of estranged labor reaches its height only through this: 1. that on the one side *labor-for-gain*, the product of the worker, stands in no *immediate* relation to his need and to his *determination as laborer*, but is determined on both sides by social combinations alien to the worker; 2. that he who *heaps up* the product does not himself produce, but exchanges what another has produced. In that crude form of *alienated* private property, of *barter*, each of the two private proprietors has produced what his need, his disposition and the available natural material immediately drove him to. Each therefore exchanges with the other only the surplus of his production. Labor was indeed his immediate *source of subsistence*, but at the same time also the exercise of his *individual existence*. Through exchange his *labor* has become in part a *source of gain*. Its purpose and its existence have become different. The product is produced as *value*, as *exchange value*, as *equivalent*, no longer on account of its immediate personal relation to the producer. The more manifold production becomes, the more manifold therefore on the one hand the needs, the more one-sided on the other hand the achievements of the producer become, the more does his labor fall into the category of *labor-for-gain*, until finally it has only this significance, and it becomes wholly *accidental* and *inessential* whether the producer stands in the relation of immediate enjoyment and personal need to his product, and also whether the *activity*, the action of labor itself, is for him self-enjoyment of his personality, the actualization of his natural dispositions and spiritual purposes.

In *labor-for-gain* there lies:

1\. The estrangement and contingency of labor from the laboring subject;

2\. the estrangement and contingency of labor from its *object*;

3\. the determination of the worker by social needs, which are alien to him and a compulsion, to which he submits out of egoistic need, out of want, and which for him have only the significance of a source of satisfaction for his necessities, just as he exists for them only as a slave of their needs;

4\. that for the worker the preservation of his individual existence appears as the *end* of his activity and his actual doing figures for him only as a means; that he exercises his life in order to acquire means of life.

The greater, the more developed, therefore, the social power appears within the relation of private property, the more *egoistic*, the more asocial, the more estranged from his own essence does man become.

As the reciprocal exchange of the products of *human activity* appears as *barter*, as *huckstering*, so the reciprocal complementation and exchange of activity itself appears as: *division of labor*, which makes of man as far as possible an abstract being, a lathe, etc., and transforms him into a spiritual and physical monstrosity.

It is precisely the *unity* of human labor that is regarded only as *division*, because the social essence comes into existence only as its opposite, in the form of estrangement. With civilization the *division of labor* increases.

Within the presupposition of the division of labor, the product, the material of private property, acquires for the individual more and more the significance of an *equivalent*; and since he no longer exchanges his *surplus*, but the object of his production can be simply *indifferent* to him, he also no longer exchanges his product directly for the being *in need of it*. The equivalent receives its existence as equivalent in *money*, which is now the immediate result of labor-for-gain and the *mediator* of exchange. (See above.)

In *money*, the complete indifference both to the nature of the material, to the specific nature of private property, and to the personality of the private proprietor, the complete dominion of the estranged thing (*Sache*) *over* man has come into appearance. What was dominion of person over person is now the universal dominion of the *thing* over the *person*, of the product over the producer. Just as already in the *equivalent*, in value, there lay the determination of the *alienation* of private property, so *money* is the sensuous, itself objective existence of this *alienation*.

||XXX| It goes without saying that political economy can comprehend this whole development only as a factum, as the offspring of accidental want.

The separation of labor from itself = separation of the worker from the capitalist = separation of labor and capital, whose original form falls apart into *landed property* and *movable*[^8] property … The original determination of private property is monopoly; as soon as it gives itself a political constitution, this is the constitution of monopoly. Completed monopoly is competition. – For the political economist, *production, consumption* and, as mediator of both, *exchange* or *distribution* fall apart. The separation of production and consumption, of activity and spirit[^9] among different individuals and within the same individual, is the *separation of labor* from its *object* and from itself as a spirit. *Distribution* is the self-exercising power of private property. – The separation of labor, capital and landed property from one another, as that of labor from labor, of capital from capital and of landed property from landed property, finally the separation of labor from wages, of capital from profit and of profit from interest, finally of landed property from ground-rent, lets self-estrangement appear both in the form of self-estrangement and in that of reciprocal estrangement.

“Let us now suppose the case that the government wants to fix the increase and decrease of money. If it strives to keep the quantity of money below the measure that the free course of things would produce, it raises the value of coined gold and makes it the interest of all to convert bars into coin. Then clandestine manufacture arises; the government must prevent it by punishments. If the government wants to keep the quantity of gold *above* the necessary height, it presses down its value; everyone seeks to cast it into bars, against which it again has only the means of punishment. But the hope of gain triumphs over the fear of punishment.” p. 137, 138.

IV\. *De la consommation.[^14]*

“*Production, distribution, échange[^15]* are mere *means*. One does not produce in order to produce. They are intermediary, mediating operations. The purpose is *consumption*.” p.237.

§ 1. “Consumption is: 1. *productive*. It comprises everything that is expended for the purpose of producing a thing, including the maintenance of the worker… then the machines, tools, buildings, animals for the productive operations; finally: the raw materials, whether the object to be produced is formed immediately out of them or whence one can draw it.” p.238, 239. “Only the things of the 2nd class are not completely consumed in the course of the productive operations.” I.c.

2\. *unproductive* consumption. “Wages of a lackey: every consumption that does not take place for the purpose of products, in order to obtain by means of one thing another equivalent, is unproductive.” p.240. “Productive consumption is itself a *means*, namely a means of production; unproductive consumption is no means, it is the end, the *enjoyment* that consumption procures, the *motive* of all preceding operations.” p.241. “Through the first kind of consumption nothing is lost; through the 2nd everything.” I.c. “What one consumes *productively* is always *capital*. That is a particularly remarkable property of productive consumption. What is consumed productively *is* capital, and it *becomes* capital through consumption.” p. 241, 242. “The totality of what the productive forces of a country create in one year is the *produit annuel brut*[^16]. The greater part of it is destined for the replacement of the consumed capital. What remains of the *produit brut*[^17] after the replacement of capital is the *produit net*[^18]; it distributes itself only as profit of capital or ground-rent.” p. 242, 243. “It is the *fonds* from which all addition to national capital usually comes.” l.c. “To *productive* and *unproductive consumption* correspond *productive* and *unproductive labor*.” p.244.

§ 2. “Everything that is produced in the course of a year is consumed in the course of the following year, productively or unproductively.” p. 246.

§ 3. “Consumption extends itself according to the measure of production; a man produces only because he demands *to have*. If the object produced is what he wants to have, then, when he has procured as much as he needs, he stops working. If he produces more, it happens because he wants to have some other object in exchange for this more. He produces the one thing out of desire to possess the other. The production of this thing is for him the only means of obtaining the other thing, and he obtains it more cheaply than if he were compelled to produce it himself. In the division of labor he restricts himself to producing a particular thing or only a part of it; only a small part of his own production does he apply to himself; the rest he destines for buying all the other commodities he demands; and when a man restricts himself to the production of a single thing and exchanges his product for all others, one finds that each receives more of the various things he desires than he would have received if he himself ||XXXI| had wanted to produce them. If a man produces for himself, *exchange* does not take place. He demands nothing to buy and offers nothing for sale. He possesses an object, he has produced it and has no intention of disposing of it. If one applies the terms “offre und demande”[^19] to this case as a metaphor, then offre and demande are here completely proportioned to one another. As regards the offre and demande of saleable objects, we can leave entirely out of question the part of the annual product that each producteur consumes in the form in which he produces or receives it.” p.251.

“If we speak here of offre and demande, we speak in general. If we say of a particular nation at a particular epoch that its offre = its demande, we do not say this in relation to 1 or 2 commodities; we want to say that its demande of all commodities taken en masse = all that it can offer in commodities of every kind. Despite this equality of offre and demande, taken in general, it can very well happen that one has produced too much or too little of one or more particular commodities in relation to the demand for these commodities.” p.251, 252. “Two things are necessary to constitute a *demand* (demande): the desire to have a commodity, and the possession of an equivalent object that one can give in exchange. A demande denotes the *desire* and the *means* to buy. If one or the other is lacking, the purchase cannot take place. The possession of an equivalent object is the necessary basis of every demand. In vain does a man wish to possess any objects if he has nothing to give in order to obtain them. The equivalent object that a man brings forward is the *instrument* of demand. The extent of his demand is measured by the value of this object. Demand and the equivalent object are the termini that one can substitute *one for the other*. One has already seen that every man who produces strives for the possession of objects other than that for whose production he has competed, and that the extent of this striving, of this desire, is *measured* by the totality of his production, insofar as he does not want to keep it for his own consumption. It is equally evident that a man can give in exchange for other objects what he has produced and does not want to consume himself. His *will to buy* and his *means of doing so* are therefore equal, or his demand is exactly = his total product, insofar as he does not want to consume it himself.” p.252, 253.

Mill here analyzes exchange on the basis of private property with his customary cynical acuity and clarity.

Man – this is the fundamental presupposition of private property – *produces* only in order *to have*. The purpose of production is *having*. And production has not merely such a *useful* purpose; it has an *egoistic* purpose; man produces only in order to *have* for himself; the object of his production is the objectification of his *immediate*, egoistic *need*. Man, for himself – in the wild, barbarous state – therefore has the measure of his production in the *extent* of his immediate need, whose content is *immediately* the produced object itself.

In this state, therefore, man produces *no more* than he immediately needs. The *limit of his need* is the *limit of his production*. Demand and supply therefore coincide exactly. His production is *measured* by his need. In this case no exchange takes place, or exchange reduces itself to the exchange of his labor for the product of his labor, and this exchange is the latent form, germ[^20], of actual exchange.

As soon as exchange takes place, surplus production takes place beyond the immediate limit of possession. This surplus production, however, is no elevation above egoistic need. It is rather only a *mediated* way of satisfying a need that finds its objectification not immediately in this production but in the production of another. Production has become a *source of gain*, labor-for-gain. While therefore in the first relation need is the measure of production, in the 2nd relation production, or rather the *possession of the product*, is the measure of how far the needs can be satisfied.

I have produced for myself and not for you, as you have produced for yourself and not for me. The result of my production has in and for itself just as little relation to you as the result of your production has an immediate relation to me. That is to say, our production is[^21] no production of man for man as man, i.e. no *social* production. As man, therefore, neither of us has a relation of enjoyment to the product of the other. As men we do not exist for our reciprocal productions. Our exchange therefore cannot be the mediating movement in which it would be confirmed that my product ||XXXII| \[is\] for you, because it is an *objectification* of your own essence, of your need. For it is not the *human essence* that is the bond of our productions for one another. Exchange can only set in *motion*, can only confirm the *character* that each of us has toward his own product, and therefore toward the production of the other. Each of us sees in his product only his *own* objectified self-interest, and therefore in the product of the other an *other*, independent of him, alien, objective self-interest.

To be sure, as man you have a human relation to my product: you have the *need* for my product. It is therefore present for you as the object of your desire and will. But your need, your desire, your willing are impotent need, desire, willing for my product. That is to say, your *human* essence, which therefore stands necessarily in an inner relation to my human production, is not your *power*, your property in this production, for the *peculiarity*, the *power* of the human essence is not recognized in my production. Rather, they are the *bond* that makes you dependent on me, because they place you in a dependence on my product. Far from being the *means* that would give you *power* over my production, they are rather the *means* of giving me power over you.

If I produce *more* than I can immediately use of the produced object myself, my surplus production is *calculated* upon your need and is refined. I produce a surplus of this object only in *semblance*. In truth I produce an *other* object, the object of your production, which I intend to exchange for this more, an exchange that I have already completed in thought. The *social* relation in which I stand to you, my labor for your need, is therefore also a mere *semblance*, and our reciprocal complementation is likewise a mere *semblance*, whose basis is reciprocal plundering. The intention of *plundering*, of *fraud*, necessarily lies in ambush, for since our exchange is an egoistic one, on your side as on mine, since each self-interest seeks to outbid the other's, we necessarily seek to deceive one another. The measure of power that I grant to my object over yours certainly requires your *recognition* in order to become an actual power. But our reciprocal recognition of the reciprocal power of our objects is a struggle, and in struggle the one who possesses more energy, force, insight or skill wins. If physical force suffices, I plunder you directly. If the realm of physical force is broken, we seek mutually to delude one another with a semblance, and the most skillful cheats the other. Whoever cheats the other is, for the *whole* of the relation, an accident. The *ideal, intended* cheating takes place on both sides, i.e. each of the two has, in his own judgment, cheated the other.

Exchange therefore necessarily mediates itself on both sides through the *object* of reciprocal production and reciprocal possession. The ideal relation to the reciprocal objects of our production is indeed our reciprocal need. But the *real* relation, the one that posits itself in *actuality*, the *true* relation that carries itself out, is only the reciprocal *exclusive possession* of reciprocal production. What gives your need for my thing a *value*, a *dignity*, an *effect* for me is solely your *object*, the *equivalent* of my object. Our reciprocal product is therefore the *means*, the *mediation*, the *instrument*, the *recognized power* of our reciprocal needs over one another. Your *demand* and the *equivalent of your possession* are therefore *synonymous*, equally valid termini for me, and your demand only has a sense and an effect when it has sense and effect in relation to me. As mere man, without this instrument, your demand is an unsatisfied striving on your part, a non-existent notion for me. You as man therefore stand in no relation to my object, because *I myself* have no human relation to it. But the *means* is the *true power* over an object, and therefore we mutually regard our product as the *power* of each over the other and over himself, i.e. our own product has risen up on its hind legs against us; it seemed to be our property, but in truth we are its property. We ourselves are excluded from *true* property, because our *property* excludes the other man.

Our objects in their relation to one another are the only intelligible language we speak to one another. A human language we would not understand, and it would remain without effect; it would be known and felt on one side as a request, as an entreaty ||XXXIII| and therefore as a *humiliation*, and hence brought forward with shame, with the feeling of being cast away, while on the other side it would be received and rejected as *impudence* or *madness*. So much are we reciprocally estranged from the human essence that the immediate language of this essence appears to us as a *violation of human dignity*, whereas the estranged language of thingly (*sachlich*) values appears as justified, self-confident and self-recognizing human dignity.

To be sure: in your eyes your product is an *instrument*, a *means* for taking possession of my product and therefore for satisfying your need. But in my eyes it is the *purpose* of our exchange. You figure for me rather as means and instrument for the production of this object, which is a purpose for me, just as conversely, in this relation, you figure as my object. But 1. each of us *actually does* what the other regards him as. You have actually made yourself a means, an instrument, the producer of *your own* object, in order to take possession of mine; 2. your own object is for you only the *sensuous envelope*, the *hidden form* of my object; for its production *signifies*, wants to *express*: the *acquisition* of my object. Thus you have in fact become for yourself a *means*, an *instrument* of your object, whose *bondsman* your desire is, and you have performed bondsman's service so that the object of your desire may never again grant you a favor. If, at the beginning of development, this reciprocal bondage of the object over us now indeed appears as the relation of *lordship* and *slavery*, that is only the *crude* and frank expression of our *essential* relation.

Our *reciprocal* value is for us the *value* of our reciprocal objects. Therefore man himself is reciprocally *worthless* to us.

Suppose we had produced as men: each of us would have *doubly affirmed* himself and the other in his production. I would have 1. in my *production* objectified my *individuality*, its *peculiarity*, and therefore enjoyed both, during the activity, an individual *manifestation of life (Lebensäußerung)*, and, in the contemplation of the object, the individual joy of knowing my personality as an *objective*, *sensuously contemplable* and therefore *raised above all doubt* power. 2. In your enjoyment or your use of my product I would have had *immediately* the enjoyment, both of the consciousness of having satisfied a *human* need in my labor, and therefore of having objectified the *human* essence and thus provided the need of another *human* being with its corresponding object, 3. of having been for you the *mediator* between you and the species, and therefore of being known and felt by you as a complement of your own essence and as a necessary part of yourself, thus of knowing myself confirmed both in your thought and in your love, 4. of having immediately created your manifestation of life in my individual manifestation of life, and therefore of having immediately confirmed and actualized in my individual activity my true essence, my *human*, my *communal being*.

Our productions would be as many mirrors from which our essence would shine back to us.

This relation thereby becomes reciprocal: let what happens on my side happen on yours.

Let us consider the different moments as they appear in the supposition:

My labor would be *free manifestation of life*, hence *enjoyment* of *life*. Under the presupposition of private property it is *alienation of life (Lebensentäußrung)*, for I work in order *to live*, in order to procure for myself a *means* of life. My working *is not* life.

Secondly: in labor there would therefore lie the *peculiarity* of my individuality, because my *individual* life is affirmed. Labor would therefore be *true, active property*. Under the presupposition of private property my individuality is alienated to the point that this *activity* is *hateful* to me, a *torment*, and rather only the *semblance* of an activity, therefore also only a *forced* activity, imposed on me only by an *external* accidental want, *not* by an *inner necessary* want.

Only as what my labor is can it appear in my object. It cannot appear as what it *is not* in essence. Therefore it appears only as the objective, sensuous, contemplated and therefore raised above all doubt expression of my *self-loss* and my *impotence*. |XXXIII||[^22]
