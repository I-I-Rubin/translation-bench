# Karl Marx: Excerpts from James Mill's Book “Élémens d'économie politique”

*Trad. par J. T. Pariser, Paris 1823*[^1]

\[Written in the first half of 1844. From the manuscript. According to Marx Engels Werke, Volume 40, Berlin 1985 {or Supplementary Volume, Part One, Berlin 1968}, pp. 443-463\]

|XXV| In that compensation of money and metal value, as in the presentation of production costs as the sole moment in the determination of value, Mill – like the whole school of Ricardo – commits the error of stating the *abstract law* without the change or the constant abolition of this law – through which it first becomes law. If it is a *constant* law that, for instance, production costs in the last instance – or rather, in the sporadically and accidentally[^2] occurring coincidence of demand and supply – determine the price (value)[^3], then it is an equally *constant* law that this relation does not coincide, i.e. that value and production costs stand in no necessary relation. Indeed, demand and supply coincide only momentarily, through the preceding fluctuation of demand and supply, through the disproportion between production costs and exchange value, just as this fluctuation and this disproportion in turn follow the momentary coincidence. This *actual* movement, of which that law is only an abstract, accidental, and one-sided moment, is made by modern political economy into an accident, into the inessential. Why? Because with the sharp and exact formulas to which they reduce political economy, the basic formula – if they wanted to express that movement abstractly – would have to read: The law in political economy is determined by its opposite, lawlessness. The true law of political economy is *chance*, from whose movement we, the scientists, arbitrarily fix a few moments in the form of laws. -

It is very good, and brings the essence of the matter into a concept, when Mill designates *money* as the *mediator* of exchange. The essence of money is not, in the first place, that property is alienated in it, but that the *mediating activity* or movement, the *human*, social act through which the products of man mutually complement one another, becomes *estranged* and acquires the character of a *material thing* outside man, namely money. In that man himself alienates this mediating activity, here he acts only as a man who has lost himself, a dehumanized man; the *relation* of the *things* (*Sachen*) themselves, the human operation with them, becomes the operation of a being outside man and above man. Through this *alien mediator* – instead of man himself being the mediator for man – man looks upon his will, his activity, his relation to others as a power independent of him and of them. His slavery thus reaches its peak. That this *mediator* now becomes the *actual God* is clear, for the mediator is the *actual power* over that with which it mediates me. Its cult becomes an end in itself. The *objects*, separated from this mediator, have lost their value. Thus only insofar as they *represent it* do they have value, whereas it originally seemed that it had value only insofar as *it* represented *them*. This reversal of the original relation is necessary. This *mediator* is therefore the self-lost, estranged *essence* of private property, the private property that has become external to itself, *alienated* (*entäußerte*) private property, just as it is the *alienated* mediation of human production with human production, the *alienated* species-activity of man. All the properties that accrue to this activity in the production of this activity are therefore transferred to this mediator. Man thus becomes the poorer as man, i.e. separated from this mediator, as this mediator becomes *richer*. -

Christ originally *represents*: 1. men before God; 2. God for men; 3. men to men.

Thus money, in its concept, originally *represents*: 1. private property for private property; 2. society for private property; 3. private property for society.

But Christ is the *alienated* God and the alienated *man*. God has value only insofar as he is Christ; man has value only insofar as he represents Christ. The same holds for money. -

Why must private property pass over into *money-essence (Geldwesen)*? Because man, as a *sociable being*, must pass over into *exchange* ||XXV|[^4], and because exchange – given the presupposition of private property – must pass over into *value*. The mediating movement of the exchanging man is namely not a social, not a human movement, not a *human relation*; it is the *abstract relation* of private property to private property, and this *abstract* relation is *value*, whose actual existence as value is first *money*. Because the exchanging men do not relate to one another as men, the *thing* (*Sache*) loses the significance of human, personal property. The social relation of private property to private property is already a relation in which private property is estranged from itself. The self-subsisting existence of this relation, money, is therefore the alienation of private property, the abstraction from its *specific*, personal nature. —

The opposition of modern political economy to the monetary system, *Système monétaire*, therefore cannot, despite all its cleverness, bring us to any decisive victory; for if the crude political-economic superstition of the people and the governments clings to the *sensuous, tangible, conspicuous* money-bag, and therefore believes in the absolute value of the noble metals and in their possession as the sole reality of wealth – if then the enlightened, worldly-wise political economist comes and proves to them that money is a commodity like any other, whose value therefore, like that of every other commodity, depends on the relation of production costs to demand, competition[^5] and supply, to the quantity or competition of the other commodities – then it is rightly answered to this political economist that the *actual* value of things is nonetheless their *exchange value*, and that this exists in the last instance in money, as money exists in the noble metals, so that money is the *true* value of things and therefore the most desirable thing. For the teachings of the political economist themselves ultimately come down to this wisdom, except that he possesses the capacity for abstraction to recognize this existence of money under all forms of commodities, and therefore does not believe in the exclusive value of its official metallic existence. – The metallic existence of money is merely the official, perceptible expression of the money-soul that lives in all the members of the production and movements of bourgeois society. -

The opposition of the modern political economists to the monetary system is only that they have grasped the *money-essence (Geldwesen)* in its abstraction and generality, and are therefore enlightened about the *sensuous* superstition that believes in the exclusive existence of this essence in the noble metal. They put the refined superstition in place of this crude one. But because both have one root in the essence, the enlightened form of superstition does not succeed in entirely displacing its crude sensuous form, since it attacks not its essence but only the particular form of this essence. – The *personal* existence of money as money – and not merely as the inner, self-subsisting, hidden intercourse relation, or *status relation*, of commodities to one another – this existence corresponds all the more to the essence of money the more abstract it is, the less *natural* relation it has to the other commodities, the more it appears as a product and yet again as a non-product of man, the less *naturally grown* its element of existence, the more it is created by man; or, in the language of political economy, the greater the *inverse* relation of its *value as money* to the exchange value or money value of the material in which it exists. Therefore *paper money* and the number of *paper representatives of money* (such as bills of exchange, mandates, promissory notes, etc.) are the *more perfect* existence of *money as money* and a necessary moment in the progress of the development of the money-essence. In the *credit system*, whose complete expression is the *banking system (Bankwesen)*, it acquires the semblance that the power of the alien, material power has been broken, the relation of self-estrangement abolished, and man restored to human relations with man. The *Saint-Simonists*, deceived by this *semblance*, regard the development of money, bills of exchange, paper money, paper representatives of money, *credit, banking system* as a gradual abolition of the separation of man from the *thing* (*Sache*), of capital from labor, of private property from money and of money from man, of the separation of man from man. The organized *banking system* is therefore their ideal. But this abolition of the ||XXVI| estrangement, this *return* of man to himself and therefore to the other man, is only a *semblance*; it is a self-estrangement, a dehumanization, all the more infamous and extreme, since its element is no longer commodity, metal, paper, but the *moral* existence, the *sociable* existence, the *inner* being of the human breast itself; since under the semblance of man's *trust* in man, it is the highest *distrust* and complete estrangement. What constitutes the essence of *credit*? We here entirely abstract from the *content* of credit, which is again money. We thus abstract from the *content* of this trust, by which one man *recognizes* another in that he advances him values and – in the best case, when he does not insist on repayment of the credit, i.e. is no usurer – bestows upon his fellow man the trust that he is not a rogue but a “good” man.

By a “good” man the trusting party here understands, as with Shylock, a “solvent” man. – Credit is conceivable under 2 relations and under 2 different conditions. The 2 relations are: first, a rich man extends credit to a poor man whom he holds to be industrious and orderly. This kind of credit belongs to the romantic, sentimental part of political economy, to its aberrations, excesses, *exceptions*, not to the *rule*. But even granting this exception, granting this romantic possibility, the life of the poor man and his talent and activity serve the rich man as a *guarantee* of the repayment of the lent money; that is to say, all the social virtues of the poor man, the content of his life activity, his very existence, represent for the rich man the reimbursement of his capital with the usual interest. The death of the poor man is therefore for the creditor the worst case. It is the death of his capital along with its interest. Consider what baseness lies in the *valuation* of a man in *money*, as it takes place in the credit relation. It goes without saying that the creditor, besides the *moral* guarantees, also has the guarantee of *juridical* coercion and, more or less, *real* guarantees for his man. If now the one to whom credit is extended is himself wealthy, then *credit* becomes merely a facilitating *mediator* of exchange; that is, *money* itself is raised into a wholly *ideal* form. *Credit* is the *political-economic* judgment on a man's *morality*. In credit, instead of metal or paper, the *man* himself has become the *mediator* of exchange, but not as a man: rather as the *existence of a capital* and of interest. The medium of exchange has thus indeed returned and been transposed from its material shape into man, but only because man himself, placed outside himself in himself, has become a material shape. It is not that money is abolished in man within the credit relation, but that man himself is transformed into *money*, or money is *incorporated* in him. *Human individuality*, human *morality*, has become both itself an article of trade and the *material* in which money exists. Instead of money, paper, my own personal existence, my flesh and blood, my sociable virtue and standing, is the matter, the body of the *money-spirit*. Credit no longer separates the money value into money, but into human flesh and human heart. So far are all advances and inconsistencies within a false system the highest regression and the highest consequence of baseness. – Within the credit system, its man-estranged nature manifests itself under the semblance of the highest political-economic recognition of man in a twofold way:

1\. The opposition between capitalist and laborer, large and small capitalist, grows even greater, in that credit is given only to him who already has, and is a new chance of accumulation for the rich; or in that the poor man, in the accidental whim and judgment of the rich man about him, sees his *entire* existence confirmed or denied, sees it wholly dependent on this accident;

2\. in that mutual representation, pretense, and hypocrisy are pushed to their peak, so that over the creditless man there is added to the simple judgment that he is poor the demoralizing judgment that he possesses no trust, no recognition, that he is thus a sociable pariah, a bad man; and in that the poor man receives, along with his privation, this humiliation and the humiliating *entreaty* for credit from the rich man; ||XXVII|

3\. in that, through this wholly *ideal* existence of money, counterfeiting can be practiced by man on no other material but only on his own person; he himself must make himself a false coin, obtain credit by deceit, lie his way to it, etc.; and this credit relation – on the side of the trusting party as well as of the one who needs trust – becomes an object of trade, an object of mutual fraud and abuse. Here, brilliantly, *distrust* reveals itself as the basis of this political-economic trust; the distrustful weighing of whether credit should be granted or not; the espionage into the secrets of the private life etc. of the applicant for credit; the betrayal of momentary difficulties in order to overthrow a rival by suddenly shaking his credit etc. The whole system of bankruptcy, the sham enterprises etc. … In *state credit* the state has entirely the same position that man held above … In the play with state paper it shows how it has become a plaything of the merchants etc.

4\. The *credit system* finds its completion in the *banking system (Bankwesen)*. The creation of the bankers, the state dominion of the bank, the concentration of wealth in these hands, this political-economic *Areopagus* of the nation, is the worthy completion of the money-essence (*Geldwesen*). In that in the credit system the *moral recognition of a man*, as well as *trust in the state* etc., has received the form of *credit*, there emerges the secret that lies in the lie of moral recognition, the *immoral* baseness of this morality, as well as the hypocrisy and egoism in that trust in the state, and it shows itself as what it actually is.

The *exchange* both of human activity within production itself, and of *human products* with one another, is = the *species-activity* and species-spirit, whose actual, conscious, and true existence is *social* activity and *social* enjoyment. Since the *human* essence is the *true communal being (Gemeinwesen)* of men [is], men *create*, produce through the exercise of their *essence* the human *communal being (Gemeinwesen)*, the social essence, which is not an abstract-universal power over the single individual, but is the essence of each individual, his own activity, his own life, his own spirit, his own wealth. It is therefore not through reflection that this *true communal being (Gemeinwesen)* arises; it therefore appears produced through the *want* and the *egoism* of individuals, i.e. immediately through the exercise of their very existence. It does not depend on man whether this communal being (*Gemeinwesen*) is or is not; but as long as man does not recognize himself as man, and therefore has not organized the world humanly, this *communal being (Gemeinwesen)* appears under the form of *estrangement*. \\

Because its *subject*, man, is a self-estranged being. Men, not in an abstraction but as actual, living, particular individuals, *are* this being. *As* they are, so is it itself. It is therefore an identical proposition that *man* estranges himself, and that the *society* of this estranged man is the caricature of his *actual communal being (Gemeinwesen)*, of his true species-life; that therefore his activity appears as torment, his own creation as an alien power over him, his wealth as poverty, the *essential bond* that binds him to other men as an inessential bond, and rather the separation from other men as his true existence; that his life appears as the sacrifice of his life, that the actualization of his essence appears as the de-actualization of his life, that his production appears as production of his nothingness, that his power over the object appears as the power of the object over him; that he, the lord of his creation, appears as the bondsman of this creation.

Now political economy comprehends the *communal being (Gemeinwesen)* of *man*, or his self-exercising *human essence*, their reciprocal complementation as species-life, as truly human life, under the form of *exchange* and *trade*. *Society*, says Destutt de Tracy, is a *series* *of mutual échanges.*[^6]* It is precisely this movement of mutual integration. *Society*, says Adam Smith, is a *trading society*. Each of its members is a *merchant*.[^7]

One sees how political economy *fixes* the *estranged* form of sociable intercourse as the *essential* and *original* form, and as corresponding to the human determination.

||XXVIII| Political economy – like actual movement – proceeds from the *relation of man to man* as that of *private proprietor to private proprietor*. If man is presupposed as a *private proprietor*, i.e. as an exclusive possessor, who proves his personality through this exclusive possession and distinguishes himself from other men, and relates to them – private property is his personal, his *distinguishing*, therefore his essential existence – then the *loss* or *giving up* of private property is an *alienation of man*, as of *private property* itself. //

We hold fast here only to the latter determination. If I relinquish my private property to another, it ceases to be mine; it becomes a *thing* (*Sache*) independent of me, lying *outside* my sphere, a thing *external* to me. I thus alienate (*entäußere*) my private property. In relation to myself I thus set it as *alienated* private property. But I set it only as an *alienated thing* (*Sache*) in general; I merely cancel my *personal* relation to it; I return it to the *elemental* powers of nature, if I alienate it only in relation to myself. It becomes *alienated private property* only when it simultaneously ceases to be *my* private property, without thereby ceasing to be private property in general; that is to say, when it enters into the same relation to *another* man *outside* me that it stood in to me myself; in a word, when it becomes the *private property* of *another* man. Leaving aside the case of *violence* – how then do I come to alienate *my* private property to another man? Political economy answers correctly: out of *want*, out of *need*. The other man is also a private proprietor, but of a *different* thing (*Sache*), which I lack and which I cannot or will not do without, which seems to me a *need* for the completion of my existence and the actualization of my essence.

The bond that relates the two private proprietors to one another is the *specific nature of the object (Gegenstand)* that is the matter of their private property. The longing for these two objects, i.e. the need for them, shows each of the private proprietors, brings home to him, that apart from private property he has yet another *essential* relation to the objects, that he is not the particular being he takes himself to be, but a *total* being, whose needs stand, in the relation of *inner* property – for need for a thing is the most evident, most irrefutable proof that the thing (*Sache*) belongs to *my* essence, that its being for me, its *property*, is the property, the peculiarity of my essence – in relation to all, even to the productions of the other's labor. Both proprietors are thus driven to give up their private property, but to give it up in such a way that they simultaneously confirm private property, or give up private property within the relation of private property. Each thus alienates a part of his private property to the other.

The *social* relation, or *social* relationship, of the two private proprietors is therefore the *reciprocity* of *alienation*, alienation set on both sides, or *alienation* as the relationship of the two proprietors, whereas in simple private property alienation takes place only in relation to itself, one-sidedly.

*Exchange*, or *barter*, is therefore the social species-act, the *communal being (Gemeinwesen)*, the social intercourse and integration of men within *private property*, and therefore the *external* (*äußerliche*), the *alienated* (*entäußerte*) species-act. Precisely for this reason it appears as *barter*. It is thus likewise the opposite of the *social* relation.

Through the reciprocal alienation or estrangement of private property, *private property* itself has come to be determined as *alienated* private property. For firstly, it has ceased to be the product of labor, the exclusive, distinguishing personality of its possessor, for he has alienated it; it has departed from the possessor whose product it was, and has acquired a personal significance for the one whose product it *is not*. It has lost its personal significance for the possessor. Secondly, it has been related to another private property, been set equal to it. In its place a private property of *other* nature has stepped in, just as it itself represents the place of a private property of *other* nature. On both sides, private property thus appears as the representative of a private property of other nature, as the *equal* of an *other* natural product, and the two sides relate to each other such that each represents the existence of its *other*, and both reciprocally relate to one another as *substitutes* of themselves and of their other. The existence of private property as such has therefore become a *substitute*, an *equivalent*. Instead of its immediate unity with itself, it is now only as a relation to an *other*. As *equivalent*, its existence is no longer its own peculiar existence. It has therefore become *value* and immediately *exchange value*. Its existence as *value* is a determination of its *self* distinct from its immediate existence, *external* (*äußerliche*) to its specific essence, an *alienated* determination (*entäußerte Bestimmung*) ||XXIX| of itself, a merely *relative* existence of it.

How this *value* further determines itself is to be developed elsewhere, as well as how it becomes price.

With the relation of exchange presupposed, *labor* becomes *immediate labor-for-gain*. This relation of alienated labor reaches its height only through this: 1. on the one side, *labor-for-gain*, the product of the worker, stands in no *immediate* relation to his need or to his *vocation of labor*, but on both sides is determined by social combinations alien to the worker; 2. that he who *heaps up* the product does not himself produce, but exchanges what another has produced. In that crude form of *alienated* private property, of *barter*, each of the two private proprietors has produced that to which his need, his disposition, and the available natural material immediately drove him. Each therefore exchanges against the other only the surplus of his production. Labor was indeed his immediate *source of subsistence*, but at the same time also the exercise of his *individual existence*. Through exchange his *labor* has become partly a *source of gain*. Its purpose and its existence have become different. The product is produced as *value*, as *exchange value*, as *equivalent*, no longer for the sake of its immediate personal relation to the producer. The more manifold production becomes, the more manifold on the one hand the needs, the more one-sided on the other the achievements of the producer become, all the more does his labor fall into the category of *labor-for-gain*, until finally it has only this significance, and it becomes wholly *accidental* and *inessential* whether the producer stands in the relation of immediate enjoyment and personal need to his product, and likewise whether the *activity*, the action of labor itself, is for him self-enjoyment of his personality, the actualization of his natural endowments and spiritual aims.

In *labor-for-gain* there lies:

1\. The estrangement and contingency of labor from the laboring subject;

2\. the estrangement and contingency of labor from its *object*;

3\. the determination of the worker by social needs, which are alien to him and a compulsion, to which he submits out of egoistic need, out of want, and which for him have only the significance of a source of satisfaction for his wants, just as he exists for them only as a slave of their needs;

4\. that for the worker the preservation of his individual existence appears as the *end* of his activity, and his actual doing figures for him only as a means; that he exercises his life in order to acquire *means of life*.

The greater and more developed the social power appears within the relation of private property, the more *egoistic*, asocial, and estranged from his own essence does man become.

As the reciprocal exchange of the products of *human activity* appears as *barter*, as *huckstering*, so the reciprocal complementation and exchanging of activity itself appears as *division of labor*, which makes of man as far as possible an abstract being, a lathe etc., and transforms him into a spiritual and physical monstrosity.

It is precisely the *unity* of human labor that is regarded only as *division*, because the social essence comes into existence only as its opposite, in the form of estrangement. With civilization the *division of labor* intensifies.

Under the presupposition of the division of labor, the product, the material of private property, acquires for the individual more and more the significance of an *equivalent*; and since he no longer exchanges his *surplus*, but the *object* of his production can be simply *indifferent* to him, so he also no longer exchanges his product directly for the *needy* being. The equivalent receives its existence as equivalent in *money*, which is now the immediate result of labor-for-gain and the *mediator* of exchange. (See above.)

In *money*, the complete indifference both to the nature of the material and to the specific nature of private property, as well as to the personality of the private proprietor, the complete dominion of the estranged *thing* (*Sache*) *over* man has come into appearance. What was dominion of person over person is now the universal dominion of the *thing* (*Sache*) over the *person*, of the product over the producer. Just as already in the *equivalent*, in value, lay the determination of the *alienation* (*Entäußerung*) of private property, so *money* is the sensuous, itself objective existence of this *alienation*.

||XXX| It goes without saying that political economy can comprehend this whole development only as a factum, as the product of accidental want.

The separation of labor from itself = separation of the worker from the capitalist = separation of labor and capital, whose original form falls into *landed property* and *movable[^8] property* … The original determination of private property is monopoly; as soon as a political constitution arises, it is that of monopoly. The completed monopoly is competition. – For the political economist, *production, consumption* and, as mediator of both, *exchange* or *distribution* fall apart. The separation of production and consumption, of activity and spirit[^9] among different individuals and within the same individual, is the *separation of labor* from its *object* and from itself as a spirit. *Distribution* is the active power of private property. – The separation of labor, capital, and landed property from one another reciprocally, as well as that of labor from labor, of capital from capital, and of landed property from landed property, and finally the separation of labor from wages, of capital from profit, and of profit from interest, and finally of landed property from ground-rent, lets self-estrangement appear both in the form of self-estrangement and of reciprocal estrangement.

“Suppose now the case that the government wants to fix the increase and decrease of money. If it strives to keep the quantity of money below the measure that the free course of things would produce, it raises the value of the coined gold and makes it everyone's interest to convert the bars into coin. Then clandestine manufacture arises; the government must prevent it by penalties. If the government wants to keep the quantity of gold *above* the necessary height, it depresses its value; everyone seeks to cast it into bars, against which it again has only the means of penalty. But the hope of gain triumphs over the fear of penalty.” p. 137, 138.

IV\. *De la consommation.[^14]*

“*Production, distribution, échange[^15]* are mere *means*. One does not produce in order to produce. They are intermediary, mediating operations. The purpose is *consumption*.” p.237.

§ 1. “Consumption is: 1. *productive*. It comprises everything that is expended for the purpose of producing a thing (*Sache*), including the maintenance of the worker… then the machines, tools, buildings, animals for the productive operations; finally: the raw materials, whether the object to be produced is formed directly out of them or whence one can derive it.” p.238, 239. “Only the things (*Sachen*) of the 2nd class are not completely consumed in the course of the productive operations.” I.c.

2\. *Unproductive* consumption. “Wages of a lackey: every consumption that does not take place for the sake of products, in order to obtain through one thing (*Sache*) another equivalent, is unproductive.” p.240. “Productive consumption is itself a *means*, namely a means of production; the unproductive is no means, it is end, the *enjoyment* that consumption affords, the *motive* of all preceding operations.” p.241. “Through the first kind of consumption nothing is lost; through the second, everything.” I.c. “What one consumes *productively* is always *capital*. This is a particularly remarkable property of productive consumption. What is consumed productively *is* capital, and it *becomes* capital through consumption.” p. 241, 242. “The totality of what the productive forces of a country create in one year is the *produit annuel brut*[^16]. The greater part of it is destined for replacing the consumed capital. What remains of the *produit brut*[^17] after replacing the capital is the *produit net*[^18]; it distributes itself only as profit of capital or ground-rent.” p. 242, 243. “It is the *fonds* from which all addition to national capital usually comes.” I.c. “To *productive* and *unproductive consumption* correspond *productive* and *unproductive* labor.” p.244.

§ 2. “Everything that is produced in the course of one year is consumed in the course of the following year, productively or unproductively.” p. 246.

§ 3. “Consumption extends itself in proportion to the measure of production; a man produces only because he *wishes to have*. If the object produced is what he wants to have, then, when he has procured for himself as much as he needs, he ceases to work. If he produces more, it happens because he wants to have some other object in exchange for this surplus. He produces the one thing out of desire to possess the other. The production of this thing is for him the only means of obtaining the other thing, and he obtains it more cheaply than if he were compelled to produce it himself. In the division of labor he restricts himself to producing a particular thing or only a part of it; only a small part of his own production he applies to himself; the rest he designates for buying all the other commodities he wants; and when a man restricts himself to the production of a single thing and exchanges his product for all others, one finds that each receives more of the various things (*Sachen*) he desires than he would have received if he had wanted to produce them ||XXXI| himself. If a man produces for himself, exchange does not take place. He wants to buy nothing and offers nothing for sale. He possesses an object, he has produced it, and has no intention of disposing of it. If one applies the terms “offre et demande”[^19] to this case as a metaphor, then offre and demande are here perfectly proportioned to each other. As regards the offre and demande of the saleable objects, we may altogether leave out of account the part of the annual product that each producteur consumes in the form in which he produces or receives it.” p.251.

“If we speak here of *offre* and *demande*, we speak in general terms. If we say of a particular nation at a particular epoch that its *offre* = its *demande*, we do not say this with respect to one or two commodities; we mean that its *demande* for all commodities taken *en masse* = all that it can offer in commodities of every kind. Despite this equality of offer and demand, taken in general, it may very well happen that one has produced too much or too little of one or more particular commodities in relation to the demand for these commodities.” p.251, 252. “Two things (*Sachen*) are necessary to constitute a *demand* (*demande*): the desire to have a commodity, and possession of an equivalent object that one can give in exchange. A *demande* denotes the *desire* and the *means* to buy. If one or the other is lacking, the purchase cannot take place. The possession of an equivalent object is the necessary basis of every demand. In vain does a man wish to possess any objects if he has nothing to give in order to obtain them. The equivalent object that a man brings is the *instrument* of his demand. The extent of his demand is measured by the value of this object. Demand and the equivalent object are the *termini* that can be substituted *one for the other*. It has already been seen that every man who produces strives for the possession of other objects than those for whose production he has competed, and that the extent of this striving, this desire, is *measured* by the totality of his production, insofar as he does not wish to keep it for his own consumption. It is equally evident that a man can give, in exchange for other objects, what he has produced and does not want to consume himself. His *will to buy* and his *means of doing so* are therefore equal, or his demand is exactly = his total product, insofar as he does not wish to consume it himself.” p.252, 253.

Here Mill analyzes exchange on the basis of private property with his habitual cynical acuity and clarity.

Man – this is the basic presupposition of private property – *produces* only in order to *have*. The purpose of production is *having*. And production has not merely such a *useful* purpose; it has a *selfish* purpose; man produces only in order to *have* for himself; the object of his production is the objectification of his *immediate*, selfish *need*. Man, for himself – in the wild, barbarous state – therefore has the measure of his production in the *extent* of his immediate need, whose content is *immediately* the object produced itself.

In this state, man therefore produces *no more* than he immediately requires. The *limit of his need* is the *limit of his production*. Demand and supply therefore coincide exactly. His production is *measured* by his need. In this case no exchange takes place, or exchange reduces itself to the exchange of his labor for the product of his labor, and this exchange is the latent form, germ[^20], of actual exchange.

As soon as exchange takes place, surplus production takes place beyond the immediate limit of possession. This surplus production, however, is no elevation above selfish need. It is rather only a *mediated* way of meeting a need that finds its objectification not immediately in this production but in the production of another. Production has become a *source of gain*, labor-for-gain. Thus, while in the first relation need is the measure of production, in the second relation production, or rather *possession of the product*, is the measure of how far the needs can be met.

I have produced for myself and not for you, just as you have produced for yourself and not for me. The result of my production has in itself just as little relation to you as the result of your production has an immediate relation to me. That is to say, our production is[^21] not a production of man for man as man, i.e. not a *social* production. As man, therefore, neither of us has a relation of enjoyment to the product of the other. As men we do not exist for each other's reciprocal productions. Our exchange therefore cannot be the mediating movement in which it would be confirmed that my product ||XXXII| \[for\] you, because it is an *objectification* of your own essence, of your need. For the *human essence* is not the bond of our productions for one another. Exchange can only set in *motion*, can only confirm the *character* that each of us has toward his own product, and so toward the production of the other. Each of us sees in his product only his *own* objectified self-interest, and so in the product of the other sees an *other*, independent of him, alien, *objective* self-interest.

To be sure, as a man you have a human relation to my product: you have the *need* for my product. It is therefore present for you as the object of your desire and will. But your need, your desire, your willing are impotent need, desire, willing for my product. That is to say, your *human* essence, which therefore stands necessarily in inner relation to my human production, is not your *power*, your property in this production, for the *peculiarity*, the *power* of the human essence is not recognized in my production. Rather they are the *bond* that makes you dependent on me, because they put you into a dependence on my product. Far from being the *means* that would give you *power* over my production, they are rather the *means* of giving me power over you.

If I produce *more* than I can immediately use of the object produced, then my surplus production is *calculated*, sophisticated, for your need. I produce only in *semblance* a surplus of this object. In truth I produce another object, the object of your production, which I intend to exchange for this surplus, an exchange that I have already accomplished in thought. The *social* relation in which I stand to you, my labor for your need, is therefore also a mere *semblance*, and our reciprocal complementation is likewise a mere *semblance*, on whose foundation reciprocal plunder serves. The intention of *plunder*, of *fraud* lies necessarily in ambush, for since our exchange is a selfish one, on your side as on mine, since every self-interest seeks to outdo the other's, we necessarily seek to cheat each other. The measure of power that I allow my object over yours does indeed, to become actual power, require your *recognition*. Our reciprocal recognition of the reciprocal power of our objects is, however, a struggle, and in the struggle he wins who possesses more energy, force, insight, or adroitness. If physical force suffices, I plunder you directly. If the realm of physical force is broken, we seek mutually to pass off a semblance on one another, and the most adroit overreaches the other. Who overreaches the other is, for the *whole* of the relation, a matter of chance. The *ideal, intended* overreaching takes place on both sides, i.e. each of the two has, in his own judgment, overreached the other.

Exchange thus mediates itself from both sides necessarily through the *object* of reciprocal production and reciprocal possession. The ideal relation to the reciprocal objects of our production is, to be sure, our reciprocal need. But the *real*, the relation that sets itself in *actuality*, the *true*, the relation that carries itself out, is only the reciprocal *exclusive possession* of reciprocal production. What gives your need for my *thing* (*Sache*) a *value*, a *worth*, an *effect* for me, is solely your *object*, the *equivalent* of my object. Our reciprocal product is thus the *means*, the *mediation*, the *instrument*, the *recognized power* of our reciprocal needs upon one another. Your *demand* and the *equivalent of your possession* are thus *synonymous*, equally valid *termini* for me, and your demand has a meaning only insofar as it has meaning and effect in relation to me. As a mere man, without this instrument, your demand is an unfulfilled striving on your part, a non-existent idea for me. You as a man thus stand in no relation to my object, because *I myself* have no human relation to it. But the *means* is the *true power* over an object, and therefore we mutually regard our product as the *power* of each over the other and over itself, i.e. our own product has risen up on its hind legs against us; it seemed our property, but in truth we are its property. We ourselves are excluded from the *true* property, because our *property* excludes the other man.

The only language we speak to each other that is comprehensible is our objects in their relation to one another. A human language we would not understand, and it would remain ineffective; from one side it would be known and felt as a request, as an entreaty ||XXXIII| and therefore as a *humiliation*, and so be put forward with shame, with a feeling of abasement; from the other side it would be received and rejected as *impudence* or *madness*. So far are we reciprocally estranged from the human essence that its immediate language appears to us as a *violation of human dignity*, whereas the estranged language of *thingly* (*sachlich*) values appears as the justified, self-confident, and self-recognizing human dignity.

To be sure: in your eyes your product is an *instrument*, a *means* for seizing my product and therefore for meeting your need. But in my eyes it is the *purpose* of our exchange. You figure for me rather as means and instrument for the production of this object, which is a purpose for me, just as, conversely, you figure in this relation as my object. But 1. each of us *actually does* what the other regards him as. You have actually made yourself a means, an instrument, the producer of *your own* object, in order to seize mine; 2. your own object is for you only the *sensuous envelope*, the *hidden form* of my object; for its production *signifies*, wants to *express*: the *acquisition* of my object. Thus you have in fact become for yourself a *means*, an *instrument* of your object, whose *bondsman* your desire is, and you have performed bondsman's service so that the object of your desire might never again grant you a favor. If this reciprocal bondage of the object over us now indeed appears at the beginning of development as a relation of *lordship* and *slavery*, this is only the *crude* and frank expression of our *essential* relation.

Our *reciprocal* value is for us the *value* of our reciprocal objects. Thus man himself is *worthless* to us reciprocally.

Suppose we had produced as men: each of us would, in his production, have *doubly affirmed* himself and the other. I would have, 1. in my *production* *objectified* my *individuality*, its *peculiarity*, and therefore have enjoyed, both during the activity an individual *manifestation of life (Lebensäußerung)*, and in contemplating the object the individual joy of knowing my personality as an *objective*, *sensuously contemplable* and therefore *beyond all doubt exalted* power. 2. In your enjoyment or your use of my product I would have had *immediately* the enjoyment, both of consciousness, of having met a *human* need in my labor, i.e. of having objectified the *human* essence, and therefore of having provided for the need of another *human* being its corresponding object; 3. of having been the *mediator* between you and the species for you, i.e. of being known and felt by you as a complement of your own essence and as a necessary part of yourself, thus of knowing myself confirmed both in your thought and in your love; 4. of having created immediately, in my individual manifestation of life (*Lebensäußerung*), your manifestation of life, i.e. of having immediately confirmed and actualized in my individual activity my true essence, my *human*, my *communal being (Gemeinwesen)*.

Our productions would be so many mirrors from which our essence would shine back at us.

This relation becomes reciprocal: let what happens on your side be what \[happens\] on mine.

Let us consider the various moments as they appear in the supposition:

My labor would be *free manifestation of life (Lebensäußerung)*, hence *enjoyment* of *life*. Under the presupposition of private property it is *alienation of life (Lebensentäußrung)*, for I work in order *to live*, in order to procure for myself a *means* of life. My working *is not* living.

Secondly: in labor, therefore, would be the *peculiarity* of my individuality, because my *individual* life is affirmed. Labor would thus be *true, active property*. Under the presupposition of private property my individuality is *alienated* (*entäußert*) to the point that this *activity* is *hateful* to me, a *torment*, and rather only the *semblance* of an activity, therefore also only a *forced* activity, imposed on me only by an *external* (*äußerliche*) contingent want, *not* by an *inner necessary* want.

Only as what my labor is can it appear in my object. It cannot appear as what it is *not* in essence. Therefore it appears only as the objective, sensuous, contemplated, and therefore beyond all doubt exalted expression of my *self-loss* and my *impotence*. |XXXIII||[^22]
