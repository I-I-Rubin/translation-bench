# Karl Marx: Excerpts from James Mill's Book “Élémens d'économie politique”

*Trad. par J. T. Pariser, Paris 1823*[^1]

\[Written in the first half of 1844. From the manuscript. Following Marx Engels Werke, volume 40, Berlin 1985 {or Supplementary Volume, Part One, Berlin 1968}, pp. 443–463\]

|XXV| In that equalization of the value of money and the value of the metal, as in the presentation of the costs of production as the sole moment in the determination of value, Mill — like the Ricardian school generally — makes the mistake of enunciating the *abstract law* without the change or constant sublation of this law — through which alone it comes to be. If it is a *constant* law that, for example, the costs of production ultimately — or rather, when demand and supply sporadically, contingently[^2] coincide — determine price (value)[^3], it is an equally *constant law* that there is no coincidence in this relationship, hence that value and the costs of production stand in no necessary relationship. Indeed, demand and supply coincide only momentarily, through the preceding fluctuation of demand and supply, through the disproportion between the costs of production and exchange value, just as this fluctuation and this disproportion likewise follow again upon their momentary coincidence. Modern political economy makes this *actual* movement, of which that law is only an abstract, contingent, one-sided moment, into an accident, into something inessential. Why? Because, given the sharp and exact formulas to which they reduce political economy, the fundamental formula, were they to enunciate that movement abstractly, would have to read: In political economy the law is determined by its opposite, lawlessness. The true law of political economy is *contingency*, from whose movement we, the scientific investigators, arbitrarily fix certain moments in the form of laws. —

Mill's designation of *money* as the *mediator* of exchange is very good: it brings the essence of the thing (*Sache*) into a concept. The essence of money is not, in the first instance, that property is alienated in it, but that the *mediating activity* or movement, the *human*, social act through which human products reciprocally complement one another, is *estranged* and becomes the property of a *material thing* outside the human being, of money. In alienating this mediating activity itself, the human being acts here only as a human being lost to himself, dehumanized; the *relation* of the things (*Sachen*) themselves, the human operation with them, becomes the operation of a being outside and above the human being. Through this *alien mediator* — instead of the human being himself being the mediator for the human being — he intuits his will, his activity, his relationship to others as a power independent of himself and of them. His slavery thus reaches its apex. That this *mediator* now becomes the *actual God* is clear, for the mediator is the *actual power* over that with which it mediates me. Its cult becomes an end in itself. Separated from this mediator, the objects have lost their value. Thus they have value only insofar as they *represent it*, whereas originally it seemed that it had value only insofar as *it represented them*. This inversion of the original relationship is necessary. This *mediator* is therefore the *essence* of private property lost to itself, estranged, private property become external to itself, *alienated*, just as it is the *alienated mediation* of human production with human production, the *alienated* species-activity of the human being. All the properties accruing to this species-activity in the production of this activity are therefore transferred to this mediator. The human being thus becomes all the poorer as a human being, i.e. separated from this mediator, the *richer* this mediator becomes. —

Christ originally *represents* 1. human beings before God; 2. God for human beings; 3. human beings to the human being.

Thus *money*, according to its concept, originally represents: 1. private property for private property; 2. society for private property; 3. private property for society.

But Christ is *alienated* God and alienated *human being*. God now has value only insofar as he represents Christ, the human being now has value only insofar as he represents Christ. Likewise with money. —

Why must private property advance to the *money-essence* (*Geldwesen*)? Because the human being, as a sociable being, must advance to *exchange* ||XXV|[^4], and because exchange — on the presupposition of private property — must advance to *value*. For the mediating movement of the exchanging human being is no social, no human movement, no *human relationship*; it is the *abstract relationship* of private property to private property, and this *abstract* relationship is *value*, whose actual existence as value is only attained in *money*. Because the exchanging human beings do not comport themselves toward one another as human beings, the *thing* (*Sache*) loses the significance of human, personal property. The social relationship of private property to private property is already a relationship in which private property is estranged from itself. The existence for itself of this relationship, money, is therefore the alienation of private property, the abstraction from its *specific*, personal nature. —

The opposition of modern political economy to the monetary system, Système monétaire, can therefore, for all its cleverness, bring us no decisive victory; for if the crude political-economic superstition of the people and governments clings to the *sensuous, palpable, conspicuous* moneybag and therefore believes in the absolute value of the precious metals and in their possession as the sole reality of wealth — if the enlightened, worldly-wise political economist then comes and proves to them that money is a commodity like any other, whose value therefore, like that of any other commodity, depends on the ratio of the costs of production to demand competition[^5] and supply, to the quantity or competition of the other commodities — this political economist is rightly answered that the *actual* value of things is nevertheless their *exchange value*, and that this ultimately exists in money, just as money exists in the precious metals; hence that money is the *true* value of things and therefore the most desirable thing. The political economist's own teachings ultimately amounted to this wisdom, except that he possesses the capacity for abstraction to recognize this existence of money under all forms of commodities and therefore not to believe in the exclusive value of its official metallic existence. — The metallic existence of money is only the official, sensuously manifest expression of the money-soul lodged in all the members of the productions and movements of civil society. —

The opposition of the modern political economists to the monetary system consists only in their having grasped the *money-essence* (*Geldwesen*) in its abstraction and universality and thus being enlightened concerning the *sensuous* superstition that believes in the exclusive existence of this essence in precious metal. They set refined superstition in the place of this crude superstition. But because both have one root in essence, the enlightened form of superstition does not manage wholly to displace its crude sensuous form, since it attacks not the latter's essence but only the determinate form of this essence. — The *personal* existence of money as money — and not merely as the inner, implicit, hidden conversational relationship, or *relationship of rank*, among commodities — this existence corresponds all the more to the essence of money the more abstract it is, the less *natural* a relationship it has to the other commodities, the more it appears as a product and yet again as a non-product of the human being, the less *naturally grown* its element of existence, the more it is created by the human being; or, in political-economic terms, the greater the *inverse* ratio of its *value as money* to the exchange value or money value of the material in which it exists. Hence *paper money* and the multitude of *paper representatives of money* (such as bills of exchange, payment orders, promissory notes, etc.) are the *more perfect* existence of *money as money* and a necessary moment in the progressive development of the money-essence (*Geldwesen*). In the *credit system* (*Kreditwesen*), whose complete expression is the *banking system* (*Bankwesen*), the semblance arises that the power of the alien, material power has been broken, the relationship of self-estrangement sublated, and the human being once again stands in human relations to the human being. Deceived by this *semblance*, the *Saint-Simonists* regard the development of money, bills of exchange, paper money, paper representatives of money, *credit, banking system* (*Bankwesen*) as a stepwise sublation of the separation of the human being from the thing (*Sache*), of capital from labor, of private property from money and of money from the human being, of the separation of the human being from the human being. The organized *banking system* (*Bankwesen*) is therefore their ideal. But this sublation of ||XXVI| estrangement, this *return* of the human being to himself and therefore to the other human being, is only a *semblance*; it is an all the more *infamous* and *extreme* self-estrangement, dehumanization, in that its element is no longer commodity, metal, paper, but *moral* existence, *sociable* existence, the *interior* of the human breast itself; in that, under the semblance of the human being's *trust* in the human being, it is the highest *distrust* and complete estrangement. What constitutes the essence of *credit*? Here we leave entirely aside the *content* of credit, which is again money. We thus leave aside the *content* of this trust, according to which one human being *recognizes* the other by advancing him values and — in the best case, namely when he does not charge for the credit, i.e. is no usurer — accords his fellow human being the trust that he is no rogue but a “good” man.

By a “good” man the person extending trust understands here, like Shylock, a “solvent” man. — Credit is conceivable under 2 relationships and under 2 different conditions. The 2 relationships are: first, a rich man extends credit to a poor man whom he considers industrious and orderly. This type of credit belongs to the romantic, sentimental part of political economy, to its aberrations, excesses, *exceptions*, not to the *rule*. Yet even presupposing this exception, conceding this romantic possibility, the poor man's life and his talent and activity figure for the rich man as a *guarantee* of the repayment of the money lent; i.e. all the poor man's social virtues, the content of his life-activity, his existence itself, represent to the rich man the reimbursement of his capital with the customary interest. The poor man's death is therefore the worst eventuality for the creditor. It is the death of his capital together with its interest. Consider the baseness involved in the *appraisal* of a human being in *money*, as occurs in the credit relationship. It goes without saying that, besides the *moral* guarantees, the creditor also has the guarantee of *juridical* coercion and further, more or less *real* guarantees for his man. If the recipient of credit is himself wealthy, *credit* becomes merely a facilitating *mediator* of exchange, i.e. *money* itself is raised into an entirely *ideal* form. *Credit* is the *political-economic* judgment upon a human being's *morality*. In credit, instead of metal or paper, the *human being* himself has become the *mediator* of exchange, but not as a human being: rather as the *existence of a capital* and its interest. The medium of exchange has thus indeed returned from its material shape into the human being and been transposed back into him, but only because the human being himself, displaced outside himself, has become within himself a material shape. Within the credit relationship, it is not money that is sublated in the human being; rather the human being himself is transformed into *money*, or money is *incorporated* in him. *Human individuality*, human *morality*, has itself become both an article of commerce and the *material* in which money exists. Instead of money, paper, my own personal existence, my flesh and blood, my sociable virtue and standing are the matter, the body of the *money-spirit*. Credit separates out money value no longer in money, but in human flesh and in the human heart. Such is the extent to which all advances and inconsistencies within a false system are the utmost regression and the utmost consistency of baseness. — Within the credit system, its nature estranged from human beings exercises itself under the semblance of the highest political-economic recognition of the human being in a twofold manner:

1\. The opposition between capitalist and worker, large and small capitalist, becomes still greater, since credit is given only to someone who already has, and is a new opportunity for accumulation for the rich; or since the poor man sees his *whole* existence affirmed or denied in the rich man's contingent discretion and judgment concerning him, sees it wholly dependent on this contingency;

2\. since reciprocal representation, dissimulation and hypocrisy are driven to their apex, so that, besides the simple judgment that he is poor, the man without credit now incurs the further, morally condemnatory judgment that he possesses no trust, no recognition, hence is a sociable pariah, a bad human being; and since, in addition to his deprivation, the poor man incurs this degradation and the degrading *request* to the rich man for credit; ||XXVII|

3\. since, through this entirely *ideal* existence of money, the human being can carry out *counterfeiting* on no other stuff, but now only on his own person: he must make himself a counterfeit coin, procure credit by stealth, by lies, etc.; and this credit relationship — on the side both of the person extending trust and of the person requiring it — becomes an article of commerce, an object of reciprocal fraud and abuse. Here *distrust* also shows itself brilliantly as the basis of this political-economic trust: the distrustful weighing of whether credit should be accorded or not; the spying into the secrets of the credit applicant's private life, etc.; the disclosure of momentary difficulties in order to bring down a rival through a sudden shaking of his credit, etc. The entire system of bankruptcy, sham enterprises, etc. … In *state credit* the state occupies precisely the same position as the human being above … Speculation in state securities shows how the state has become the plaything of the traders, etc.

4\. Finally, the *credit system* has its consummation in the *banking system* (*Bankwesen*). The creation of the bankers, the bank's dominion over the state, the concentration of wealth in these hands, this political-economic *Areopagus* of the nation, is the worthy consummation of the money-essence (*Geldwesen*). Since in the credit system the *moral recognition of a human being*, like *trust in the state*, etc., received the form of *credit*, the secret lying in the lie of moral recognition comes to light — the *immoral* baseness of this morality — as do the hypocrisy and egoism in that trust in the state, and it shows itself as what it actually is.

The *exchange* both of human activity within production itself and of *human products* for one another is = *species-activity* and species-spirit, whose actual, conscious and true existence is *social* activity and *social* enjoyment. Since the *human* essence \[is\] the *true communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*) of human beings, human beings *create*, produce, through the exercise of their *essence*, the human *communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*), the social essence, which is no abstract-universal power confronting the single individual but is the essence of each individual, his own activity, his own life, his own spirit, his own wealth. That *true communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*) therefore does not arise through reflection; it therefore appears produced through the *want* and *egoism* of individuals, i.e. immediately through the exercise of their existence itself. Whether this communal being (*Gemeinwesen*) is or is not does not depend on the human being; but so long as the human being has not recognized himself as a human being and accordingly organized the world humanly, this *communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*) appears under the form of *estrangement*. \\

Because its *subject*, the human being, is a being estranged from himself. Human beings, not in an abstraction but as actual, living, particular individuals, *are* this being. *As* they are, so therefore is it itself. It is therefore an identical proposition that the *human being* estranges himself and that the *society* of this estranged human being is the caricature of his *actual communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*), of his true species-life; that therefore his activity appears as torment, his own creation as an alien power over him, his wealth as poverty, the *bond of essence* that ties him to the other human being as an inessential bond, and, rather, separation from the other human being as his true existence; that his life appears as the sacrifice of his life, the actualization of his essence as the deactualization of his life, his production as the production of his nothingness, his power over the object as the object's power over him; that he, the lord of his creation, appears as the bondsman of this creation.

Now political economy comprehends the *communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*) of the *human being*, or their *human essence* exercising itself, their reciprocal complementation into species-life, into truly human life, under the form of *exchange* and *trade*. *Society*, says Destutt de Tracy, is a *series* *of reciprocal échanges*.[^6] It is precisely this movement of reciprocal integration. *Society*, says Adam Smith, is a *trading society*. Each of its members is a *merchant*.[^7]

One sees how political economy *fixes* the *estranged* form of sociable intercourse as the *essential* and *original* form, corresponding to the human determination.

||XXVIII| Political economy — like the actual movement — proceeds from the *relationship of the human being to the human being* as that of *private proprietor to private proprietor*. If the human being is presupposed as a *private proprietor*, i.e. as an exclusive possessor who substantiates his personality through this exclusive possession and distinguishes himself from other human beings as well as relating himself to them — private property is his personal existence, the existence that *distinguishes* him, hence his essential existence — then the *loss* or *relinquishment* of private property is an *alienation of the human being*, as of *private property* itself. //

Here we hold fast only to the latter determination. If I cede my private property to another, it ceases to be mine; it becomes a thing (*Sache*) independent of me, lying *outside* my sphere, a thing (*Sache*) *external* to me. I thus *alienate* (*entäußere*) my private property. In relation to myself, I thus set it as *alienated* private property. But if I alienate it only in relation to myself, I set it only as an *alienated* thing (*Sache*) in general, I sublate only my *personal* relationship to it, I give it back to the *elemental* powers of nature. It becomes alienated *private property* only when it simultaneously ceases to be *my* private property without thereby ceasing to be *private property* in general; i.e. when it enters into the same relationship to an *other* human being *outside* me in which it stood to me myself; in a word, when it becomes the *private property* of an *other* human being. Excepting the case of *violence* — how do I now come to alienate *my* private property to another human being? Political economy answers correctly: from *want*, from *need*. The other human being is also a private proprietor, but of an *other* thing (*Sache*), which I lack and cannot or will not do without, which seems to me a *need* for the completion of my existence and the actualization of my essence.

The bond relating the two private proprietors to one another is the *specific nature of the object* that is the matter of their private property. The longing for these two objects, i.e. the need for them, shows each private proprietor, brings to his consciousness, that besides private property he has yet another *essential* relationship to the objects; that he is not the particular being he takes himself to be, but a *total* being whose needs stand in the relationship of *inner* property to all, including the productions of the other's labor — for the need for a thing (*Sache*) is the most evident, most irrefutable proof that the thing (*Sache*) belongs to *my* essence, that its being for me, its *property*, is the property, the peculiarity of my essence. Both proprietors are thus driven to relinquish their private property, but to relinquish it so that they simultaneously confirm private property, or to relinquish private property within the relationship of private property. Each thus alienates a part of his private property to the other.

The *social* relation or *social* relationship of the two private proprietors is thus the *reciprocity* of *alienation*, the relationship of alienation set on both sides, or *alienation* as the relationship of the two proprietors, whereas in simple private property *alienation* occurs only in relation to itself, one-sidedly.

*Exchange*, or *barter*, is thus the social act, the species-act, the communal being (*Gemeinwesen*), the social intercourse and integration of human beings within *private property*, and therefore the external (*äußerliche*), the *alienated* (*entäußerte*) species-act. Precisely for this reason it appears as *barter*. It is therefore equally the opposite of the *social* relationship.

Through the reciprocal alienation or estrangement of private property, *private property* itself has entered into the determination of *alienated* private property. For, first, it has ceased to be the product of labor, the exclusive, distinguishing personality of its possessor: he has alienated it; it has passed away from the possessor whose product it was and acquired a personal significance for the person whose product it is *not*. It has lost its personal significance for the possessor. Second, it has been related to another private property, set equal to it. A private property of *other* nature has taken its place, just as it itself stands in the place of a private property of *other* nature. On both sides, therefore, private property appears as the representative of a private property of other nature, as the *equal* of an *other* natural product, and the two sides relate to one another so that each stands for the existence of its *other* and both reciprocally relate to one another as *stand-ins* for themselves and their other. The existence of private property as such has therefore become a *substitute*, an *equivalent*. In place of its immediate unity with itself, it now is only as relation to an *other*. As *equivalent*, its existence is no longer the existence peculiar to it. It has therefore become *value*, and immediately *exchange value*. Its existence as *value* is a determination of its *self* different from its immediate existence, external to its specific essence, an *alienated* determination ||XXIX| of itself, a merely *relative* existence of it.

How this *value* further determines itself is to be developed elsewhere, as is how it becomes price.

The relationship of exchange presupposed, *labor* becomes *immediate labor-for-gain*. This relationship of estranged labor reaches its height only through the following: 1. on the one side, *labor-for-gain*, the worker's product, stands in no *immediate* relationship to his need and to his *determination of labor*, but is determined on both sides by social combinations alien to the worker; 2. the person who *heaps up* the product does not himself produce, but exchanges what another has produced. In that crude shape of *alienated* private property, of *barter*, each of the two private proprietors has produced what his need, his aptitude and the available natural material immediately impelled him to produce. Each therefore exchanges with the other only the surplus of his production. Labor was indeed his immediate *source of subsistence*, but at the same time also the exercise of his *individual existence*. Through exchange his *labor* has partly become a *source of gain*. Its end and its existence have become different. The product is produced as *value*, as *exchange value*, as *equivalent*, no longer for the sake of its immediate personal relation to the producer. The more many-sided production becomes, hence the more many-sided the needs on the one hand and the more one-sided the producer's performances on the other, the more his labor falls into the category of *labor-for-gain*, until it finally has only this significance, and it becomes wholly *contingent* and *inessential* both whether the producer stands to his product in the relationship of immediate enjoyment and personal need, and whether the *activity*, the action of labor itself, is for him self-enjoyment of his personality, the actualization of his natural aptitudes and spiritual ends.

*Labor-for-gain* involves:

1\. The estrangement and contingency of labor with respect to the laboring subject;

2\. the estrangement and contingency of labor with respect to its object;

3\. the determination of the worker by social needs, which, however, are alien to him and a compulsion to which he submits from egoistic need, from want, and which have for him only the significance of a source for meeting his necessities, just as he exists for them only as a slave of their needs;

4\. that the preservation of his individual existence appears to the worker as the *end* of his activity and his actual doing figures for him only as a means; that he exercises his life in order to acquire means of life.

Thus the greater, the more developed the social power appears within the private-property relationship, the more *egoistic*, devoid of society, estranged from his own essence the human being becomes.

Just as the reciprocal exchange of the products of *human activity* appears as *barter*, as *haggling*, so the reciprocal complementation and exchange of activity itself appears as: *division of labor*, which makes the human being as far as possible into an abstract being, a lathe, etc., and transforms him to the point of mental and physical monstrosity.

Precisely the *unity* of human labor is regarded only as *division*, because the social essence comes to existence only as its opposite, in the form of estrangement. The *division of labor* increases with civilization.

Within the presupposition of the division of labor, the product, the material of private property, increasingly acquires for the single individual the significance of an *equivalent*; and just as he no longer exchanges his *surplus*, but the object of his production can be utterly *indifferent* to him, so he no longer exchanges his product immediately for the being he *needs*. The equivalent acquires its existence as equivalent in *money*, which is now the immediate result of labor-for-gain and the *mediator* of exchange. (See above.)

In *money*, the complete indifference both toward the nature of the material, toward the specific nature of private property, and toward the personality of the private proprietor, the complete dominion of the estranged thing (*Sache*) *over* the human being has entered into appearance. What was dominion of person over person is now the universal dominion of the *thing* (*Sache*) over the *person*, of the product over the producer. Just as the determination of the *alienation* of private property already lay in the *equivalent*, in value, so *money* is the sensuous, itself objective existence of this *alienation*.

||XXX| It goes without saying that political economy can comprehend this entire development only as a factum, as the offspring of contingent want.

The separation of labor from itself = separation of the worker from the capitalist = separation of labor and capital, whose original form divides into *landed property* and *movable[^8] property* … The original determination of private property is monopoly; hence, as soon as it gives itself a political constitution, this is the constitution of monopoly. Consummated monopoly is competition. — For the political economist, *production, consumption* and, as the mediator of both, *exchange* or *distribution* fall apart. The separation of production and consumption, of activity and spirit[^9], among different individuals and within the same individual, is the *separation of labor* from its *object* and from itself as a spirit. *Distribution* is the power of private property exercising itself. — The reciprocal separation of labor, capital, landed property, as well as the separation of labor from labor, capital from capital, landed property from landed property, finally the separation of labor from wages, capital from profit and profit from interest, finally of landed property from ground rent, makes self-estrangement appear both in the shape of self-estrangement and in that of reciprocal estrangement.

“Now suppose the case in which the government wishes to fix the increase and decrease of money. If it endeavors to keep the quantity of money below the measure that the free course of things would produce, it raises the value of coined gold and makes it everyone's interest to transform bullion into coin. Clandestine manufacture then arises; the government must prevent it through punishments. If the government wishes to keep the quantity of gold *above* the requisite level, it depresses its value; everyone seeks to cast it into bullion, against which the government again has only the means of punishment. But the hope of profit prevails over the fear of punishment.” pp. 137, 138.

§ 9. “If 2 individuals each owed the other £100, instead of paying this sum out to one another, they have only to exchange their obligations with one another. Likewise between 2 nations … Hence *bills of exchange*, all the more necessary at a time when unenlightened policy prohibited the *exportation* of precious metals and punished it cruelly.” p. 142 ff.

§ 10. Saving of *unproductive* consumption through paper money, p. 146 ff.

§11. “The inconveniences of paper money are: 1. The *failure”* sin[^10] (manque) “of the persons issuing paper money to fulfill their obligations. 2. Counterfeits. 3. Changes in exchange rates, altération des cours.” p. 149.

§ 12. Precious metals are commodities. One exports only commodities that are less dear in the country they come from than in the country they go to, and one imports only commodities that are dearer in the country they go to than in the country they come from. Whether \[one\] should import or export the precious metals thus depends on their value in a country. \[p. 175 ff.\]

§ 13. “The value of the precious metals is the quantity of other things (*Sachen*) given for them in exchange.” p. 177. “This relationship is different in different countries and even in different regions of the same country. … *‘La vie est moins chère’[^11]* means that at a determinate place one can buy[^12] the means of life with a smaller sum of money.” \[p. 177.\]

§ 14. “The relationship of nations is like that of merchants … ils achéteront toujours au meilleur marché possible, et vendront toujours le plus cher qu'ils pourront[^13].” p. 215.

IV\. *De la consommation.[^14]*

“*Production, distribution, échange[^15]* are mere *means*. One does not produce in order to produce. They are intermediary, mediating operations. The end is *consumption*.” p. 237.

§ 1. “Consumption is: 1. *productive*. Comprises everything expended for the purpose of producing a thing (*Sache*), includes the worker's maintenance … then the machines, implements, buildings, animals for the productive operations; finally: the raw materials, whether the object to be produced is formed immediately from them or can be drawn from them.” pp. 238, 239. “Only the things (*Sachen*) of the 2nd class are not completely consumed in the course of the productive operations.” I.c.

2\. *Unproductive* consumption. “A lackey's pay, every consumption that occurs not for the sake of products, to obtain through one thing (*Sache*) another equivalent thing, is unproductive.” p. 240. “Productive consumption is itself a *means*, namely a means of production; unproductive consumption is no means, it is end, the *enjoyment* that consumption affords, the *motive* of every preceding operation.” p. 241. “Through the first type of consumption nothing is lost, through the 2nd everything.” I.c. “What is consumed *productively* is always *capital*. This is a particularly remarkable property of productive consumption. What is productively consumed *is* capital, and it *becomes* capital through consumption.” pp. 241, 242. “The totality of what a country's productive forces create in a year is the produit annuel brut[^16]. The greatest part of it is determined for the replacement of the consumed capital. What remains of the produit brut[^17] after the replacement of capital is the produit net[^18]; it is distributed only as profit of capital or ground rent.” pp. 242, 243. “It is the fonds from which all additions to the national capital ordinarily come.” l.c. “*Productive* and *unproductive* labor correspond to *productive* and *unproductive consumption*.” p. 244.

§ 2. “Everything produced in the course of one year is consumed in the course of the following year, productively or unproductively.” p. 246.

§ 3. “Consumption expands according to the measure of production; a human being produces only because he desires *to have*. If the object produced is what he wishes to have, then, once he has procured as much as he requires, he stops working. If he produces more, he does so because he wishes to have some other object in exchange for this more. He produces the one thing (*Sache*) from the desire to possess the other. Producing this thing (*Sache*) is his sole means of obtaining the other thing (*Sache*), and in this way he obtains it more cheaply than if he were compelled to produce it himself. Under the division of labor he restricts himself to producing a determinate thing (*Sache*) or only a part of it; only a small part of his own production does he apply to himself; the rest he determines for buying all the other commodities he desires; and when a human being restricts himself to producing a single thing (*Sache*) and exchanges his product for all the others, one finds that each receives more of the different things (*Sachen*) he desires than he would have received had he wished to produce them ||XXXI| himself. If a human being produces for himself, *exchange* does not occur. He desires to buy nothing and offers nothing for sale. He possesses an object, he has produced it, and has no intention of parting with it. If one applies the terms ‘offre and demande’[^19] metaphorically to this case, offre and demande are here completely proportionate to one another. As regards the offre and demande of saleable objects, we can leave entirely out of consideration the part of the annual product that each producteur consumes in the shape in which he produces or receives it.” p. 251.

“When we speak here of offre and demande, we speak in general. If we say of a determinate nation at a determinate epoch that its offre = its demande, we do not say this in relation to 1 or 2 commodities; we mean that its demande for all commodities taken en masse = everything it can offer in commodities of every type. Despite this equality of offre and demande, taken in general, it may very well happen that too much or too little of one or more particular commodities has been produced in relation to the demand for these commodities.” pp. 251, 252. “Two things (*Sachen*) are requisite to constitute a *demand* (demande): the desire to have a commodity and the possession of an equivalent object that can be given in exchange. A demande designates the *desire* and the *means* to buy. If either is lacking, the purchase cannot occur. Possession of an equivalent object is the necessary basis of every demand. In vain does a human being wish to possess any objects if he has nothing to give to obtain them. The equivalent object a human being brings with him is the *instrument* of demand. The extent of his demand measures itself by the value of this object. Demand and the equivalent object are terms that can be substituted *one for the other*. We have already seen that every producing human being strives to possess objects other than the one to whose production he has contributed, and that the extent of this striving, this desire, is *measured* by the totality of his production insofar as he does not wish to retain it for his own consumption. It is equally evident that a human being can give what he has produced and does not wish to consume himself in exchange for other objects. His *will to buy* and his *means of doing so* are thus equal, or his demand is exactly = his total product insofar as he does not wish to consume it himself.” pp. 252, 253.

Here Mill analyzes exchange on the basis of private property with his customary cynical incisiveness and clarity.

The human being — this is the fundamental presupposition of private property — *produces* only in order to *have*. The end of production is *having*. And production not only has such a *useful* end; it has a *self-interested* end; the human being produces only in order to *have* for himself; the object of his production is the objectification of his *immediate*, self-interested *need*. The human being for himself — in the wild, barbarous condition — therefore has the measure of his production in the *scope* of his immediate need, whose content is *immediately* the produced object itself.

In this condition, therefore, the human being produces *no more* than he immediately needs. The *limit of his need* is the *limit of his production*. Demand and supply therefore coincide exactly. His production is *measured* by his need. In this case no exchange occurs, or exchange reduces itself to the exchange of his labor for the product of his labor, and this exchange is the latent form, germ[^20], of actual exchange.

As soon as exchange occurs, surplus production occurs beyond the immediate limit of possession. But this surplus production is no elevation above self-interested need. Rather, it is only a *mediated* way of meeting a need that finds its objectification not immediately in this production but in the production of another. Production has become a *source of gain*, labor-for-gain. Thus, whereas in the first relationship need is the measure of production, in the 2nd relationship production, or rather *possession of the product*, is the measure of how far needs can be met.

I have produced for myself and not for you, just as you have produced for yourself and not for me. In and for itself, the result of my production has just as little relation to you as the result of your production has an immediate relation to me. I.e. our production is[^21] not production by the human being for the human being as a human being, i.e. not *social* production. As a human being, therefore, neither of us has a relation of enjoyment to the other's product. As human beings we do not exist for our reciprocal productions. Our exchange therefore cannot be the mediating movement in which it would be confirmed that my product ||XXXII| is \[for\] you because it is an *objectification* of your own essence, of your need. For the *human essence* is not the bond of our productions for one another. Exchange can only set in *movement*, only confirm, the *character* of each of us in relation to his own product and hence to the other's production. Each of us sees in his product only his *own* objectified self-interest, hence in the other's product an *other*, independent of him, alien, objective self-interest.

As a human being you do indeed have a human relation to my product: you have the *need* for my product. It therefore exists for you as the object of your desire and your will. But your need, your desire, your willing are powerless need, desire, willing for my product. I.e. your *human* essence, which therefore necessarily stands in an inner relation to my human production, is not your *power*, your property in this production, for neither the *peculiarity* nor the *power* of the human essence is recognized in my production. Rather, they are the *bond* making you dependent on me, because they place you in dependence on my product. Far from being the *means* that would give you *power* over my production, they are rather the *means* of giving me power over you.

If I produce *more* than I can immediately use myself of the object produced, my surplus production is *calculated* for your need, refined with a view to it. Only in *semblance* do I produce a surplus of this object. In truth I produce an *other* object, the object of your production, which I intend to exchange for this surplus, an exchange I have already accomplished in thought. The *social* relation in which I stand to you, my labor for your need, is therefore likewise a mere *semblance*, and our reciprocal complementation is equally a mere *semblance* founded on reciprocal plunder. The intention of *plunder*, of *fraud*, necessarily lies in ambush: since our exchange is self-interested, on your side as on mine, since each self-interest seeks to outbid the alien self-interest, we necessarily seek to defraud one another. The measure of power I accord my object over yours does indeed require your *recognition* in order to become an actual power. But our reciprocal recognition concerning the reciprocal power of our objects is a struggle, and in the struggle the victor is the one who possesses more energy, force, insight or dexterity. If physical force suffices, I plunder you directly. If the dominion of physical force is broken, we seek reciprocally to impose a semblance upon one another, and the most dexterous gets the better of the other. Which one gets the better of the other is a contingency for the *whole* relationship. The *ideal, intended* outwitting occurs on both sides, i.e. each has, in his own judgment, got the better of the other.

Exchange thus necessarily mediates itself on both sides through the *object* of reciprocal production and reciprocal possession. The ideal relationship to the reciprocal objects of our production is indeed our reciprocal need. But the *real* relationship, setting itself in *actuality*, the *true* relationship, carrying itself out, is only the reciprocal *exclusive possession* of reciprocal production. What gives your need for my thing (*Sache*) a *value*, a *dignity*, an *effect* for me is solely your *object*, the *equivalent* of my object. Our reciprocal product is thus the *means*, the *mediation*, the *instrument*, the *recognized power* of our reciprocal needs over one another. Your *demand* and the *equivalent of your possession* are thus *synonymous*, equally valid terms for me, and your demand has sense only because it has effect, only when it has sense and effect in relation to me. As a mere human being, without this instrument, your demand is an unmet striving on your part, an idea nonexistent for me. You as a human being thus stand in no relationship to my object, because *I myself* have no human relationship to it. But the *means* is the *true power* over an object, and we therefore reciprocally intuit our product as the *power* of each over the other and over himself; i.e. our own product has reared up on its hind legs against us: it seemed our property, but in truth we are its property. We ourselves are excluded from *true* property because our *property* excludes the other human being.

The only intelligible language we speak to one another is our objects in their relation to one another. A human language we would not understand, and it would remain without effect; on the one side it would be known, felt as a request, as entreaty ||XXXIII|, and therefore as a *humiliation*, and would consequently be uttered with shame, with the feeling of self-abasement; on the other side it would be received and rejected as *impudence* or *madness*. So greatly are we reciprocally estranged from the human essence that the immediate language of this essence appears to us as a *violation of human dignity*, whereas the estranged language of thingly (*sachlich*) values appears as justified, self-trusting, self-recognizing human dignity.

Certainly: in your eyes your product is an *instrument*, a *means* of taking possession of my product and hence of meeting your need. But in my eyes it is the *end* of our exchange. You figure for me, rather, as means and instrument for producing this object, which is an end for me, just as, conversely, you figure in this relationship to my object. But 1. each of us actually *does* what the other intuits him as being. You have actually made yourself the means, the instrument, the producer of *your* own object in order to take possession of mine; 2. your own object is for you only the *sensuous envelope*, the *hidden shape* of my object; for its production *signifies*, seeks to *express*: the *acquisition* of my object. Thus you have in fact become for yourself the *means*, the *instrument* of your object, whose *bondsman* your desire is, and you have performed bondsman's services so that the object of your desire may never again bestow a favor. If this reciprocal bondage to the object now actually appears at the beginning of the development as the relationship of *lordship* and *slavery*, this is only the *crude*, candid expression of our *essential* relationship.

Our *reciprocal* value is for us the *value* of our reciprocal objects. Hence the human being himself is reciprocally *valueless* for us.

Suppose we had produced as human beings: each of us would in his production have *doubly affirmed* himself and the other. I would have 1. *objectified* my *individuality*, its *peculiarity*, in my *production*, and would therefore have enjoyed both an individual *manifestation of life* (*Lebensäußerung*) during the activity and, in intuiting the object, the individual joy of knowing my personality as a power that is *objective, open to sensuous intuition*, and therefore *beyond all doubt*. 2. In your enjoyment or your use of my product I would *immediately* have the enjoyment of the consciousness of having met a *human* need in my labor, thus of having objectified the *human* essence and therefore provided the need of another *human* being with its corresponding object; 3. of having been for you the *mediator* between you and the species, thus of being known and felt by you yourself as a complement of your own essence and as a necessary part of yourself, thus of knowing myself confirmed in your thought as in your love; 4. of having immediately created your manifestation of life in my individual manifestation of life (*Lebensäußerung*), thus of having immediately *confirmed* and *actualized* in my individual activity my true essence, my *human* essence, my *communal being* (*Gemeinwesen*).

Our productions would be so many mirrors from which our essence would shine back toward us.

The relationship thereby becomes reciprocal: let what happens on my side happen on yours.

Let us consider the different moments as they appear in the supposition:

My labor would be *free manifestation of life* (*Lebensäußerung*), hence *enjoyment* of *life*. On the presupposition of private property it is *alienation of life* (*Lebensentäußrung*), for I work in order *to live*, to procure a *means* of life for myself. My working *is not* life.

Second: in labor, therefore, the *peculiarity* of my individuality would be affirmed, because my *individual* life would be affirmed. Labor would thus be *true, active property*. On the presupposition of private property my individuality is alienated to the point where this *activity* is *hateful* to me, a *torment*, and rather only the *semblance* of an activity, hence also only a *compelled* activity, imposed on me only by an *external* (*äußerliche*) contingent want, *not* by an *inner necessary* want.

My labor can appear in my object only as what it is. It cannot appear as what in essence it is *not*. Hence it appears now only as the objective, sensuous, intuited and therefore indubitable expression of my *loss of self* and my *powerlessness*. |XXXIII||[^22]
