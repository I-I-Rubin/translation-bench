# On the Logical Misery of Economics

Even so resolute a critic of the Marxian theory of value as Joan Robinson advanced in 1977 the provocative claim that “orthodox academic economics has been impoverished by its refusal to take Marx seriously”. All the doctrines which it had “developed in order to ward off Marxism” were, she said, “at present in a state of visible decay (!) (...) on account (...) also of their (...) inability to provide any instruments for coming to terms with the problems of the crisis of today (...)”. And in italics she continues: “*This deplorable state of academic teaching is largely the result of its refusal to come to grips with the questions posed by Marx*.” But her criticism was directed also at “the Marxists in the West”; with regard to their “use” of “the Marxian terminology” she reproached them with “*refusing to translate it into a language no longer exposed to the charge of unintelligibility*”.[^1]

The topicality of this provocation, twenty years old, and of the demand derived from it, seems evident. And yet a weighty objection immediately presses itself upon us: is it not an absurd enterprise to want to “translate” the Marxian “terminology” precisely into that very language, and thereby of necessity into precisely that system of categories which underlies the very teaching systems that are criticized, systems which according to her are in “decay”? Plainly a quite different system of categories underlies that “unintelligible terminology” too, a system which consequently refuses every “translation” into a theory of economics in the proper sense and into the terminology corresponding to it.

As is well known, the subtitle of Marx's major work reads *Critique of Political Economy*, and this critique was conceived from the outset in the sense of a “*critique of the economic categories*” (29/550) as such — thus not merely as a critique of this or that teaching system. A “critique of *economics* from the standpoint of economics” (2/32) seemed to Marx inadequate. In fact: as a critique conceived in principle, it had to transcend the “standpoint of economics” altogether; but how is one to represent such a “standpoint” beyond economics to oneself, and what can it meaningfully mean to want to criticize economics as a whole, as a discipline?

A passage in the third volume of *Capital* gives a notion of the consistency with which Marx carried out this programme of critique and knew how to distinguish his concepts radically from those of economics. On the occasion of a categorical differentiation which seems to academic economics so absurd that it has remained a taboo even for progressive economists — the distinction between rational (*rationell*) and “irrational (*irrationell*) prices” — he makes a statement whose fundamental significance is still overlooked: “What Hegel says with regard to certain mathematical formulae holds here: that what the common human understanding (!) finds irrational (*irrationell*) is the rational (*Rationelle*), and that what is rational for it is itself irrationality”. (25/787) Marx here declares that in his conception of theory, in “presentation” as “critique”, he has torn up the tablecloth with academic economics, that an unbridgeable abyss gapes between the systems: he knows this and expressly draws attention to the fact that his theory must present itself to the academic one as the quintessence of “irrationality”; and vice versa.

The opposition of the two systems is in fact so unbridgeable that even economists close to the Marxian theory consider it necessary to pass over in silence Marx's characterization of the prices of so-called factor services as “irrational (*irrationell*)” or “imaginary” prices — for they fear that the use of such concepts would isolate them within the normal science of economics, would catapult them out of intra-economic discourse. At this hinge of Marxian concept-formation, evidently misjudged also by Sombart, it becomes clear that one cannot conceivably be “capitalist-minded” “with Marxian science in one's body”.

Whence, then, such a concept of irrationality? And above all: in what does this irrationality express itself, and what criteria are there for being able to distinguish rationally between evidently graded forms of rationality and of irrationality? Marx refers in the quotation above directly to § 231 of the *Encyclopaedia*, in which Hegel, in the context of his critique of the “determinations of the understanding” and of the “understanding” method of the formally logical explication of concepts, grounds the necessity of their “sublation” thus: “In its course (...) it stumbles (...) finally upon *incommensurabilities* and *irrationalities* (!), where it, if it wants to go on determining, is *driven beyond* the principle of the understanding (!). Here too there enters (...) the reversal (!), whereby what is called *rational* is the work of the understanding (*das Verständige*), whereas what is called *irrational* is rather a beginning and trace of rationality (*Vernünftigkeit*) (!).”[^2] For Marx, economic thinking is a thinking shaped by the “understanding” (25/787) operating in formally logical fashion and by the antinomies and paradoxes peculiar to it — the “critique of the categories of economics” figures for him, by contrast, as a form of dialectical “reason”. Hegel's critique of the sciences “of the understanding”, that is here: of the “other” sciences, is one which Marx finds confirmed on all sides in his analysis of economic concept-formation. If he sums up his critique of “political” economy in the assertion that “the” economists “cannot utter a single proposition without contradicting themselves” (26.3/316), then this blanket judgment, strange at first sight, is of course not meant to call the capacity for thought of economists into question; nor is it directed at all against the peculiarity of economic thinking, but exclusively against it as a shape of “abstractly understanding thought”. According to Hegel this “proves itself to be the constant sublation of itself”,[^3] and it says “precisely the opposite of what” it “wanted to have said” — to critique it turns out to be mere “opinion”.[^4]

Marx takes up a “standpoint” beyond the “abstractly understanding” economics by wanting to “develop” the economic categories from the “standpoint” of the Hegelian science of reason. Hence he makes his own Hegel's critique of the “other” science, the science “of the understanding”: “Other sciences, when they come, as must necessarily (...) happen to them, to the boundary of their procedure of the understanding, help themselves in an easy way. They break off its consequence and take what they need, often the opposite of what went before, from outside, from representation, opinion, perception (...)”.[^5]

In §§ 231 and 229 of the *Encyclopaedia* further objections are to be found against the method of the “understanding” thinking of the particular sciences, which within its boundary is indispensable for Hegel as for Marx, that is, against their so-called “analytical method”[^6] — one need only compare the concepts employed in § 226 of the *Encyclopaedia* with those in *Theories of Surplus Value* (26.3/491) — a method which is precisely not to be abstractly negated, but to be sublated, even within the “genetic presentation (!)” (ibid.) of Marxian “critique” which is opposed to it. Hegel continues his critique in a way which Marx presumably lets figure without restriction only for the social sciences: “Further, in general with regard to the content of the objects defined there is no necessity present at all. One has had to put up with the fact that there is space, that there are plants, animals etc., and it is no business of geometry, botany etc. to exhibit the necessity of the objects named. (...) Philosophy has to justify itself, before all else, with regard to the necessity of its objects.”[^7]

That value, price, money, capital, interest etc. “are”, namely, that they “are given” at all, is a matter of contingent facts which are taken up “from outside, from (...) opinion, perception”. When Marx reproaches the economists with, for instance, accepting without reflection the existence of capital, he also expresses this by saying that this social existence, produced by human beings, is taken in thoughtlessly “out of experience” (42/175), is “received out of experience” (II.2/61) — that the “different sorts of capital (...) sweep in externally” (42/580) “in (!) economics”, that determinations in general come “sweeping in” (42/193 n., 26.3/363). Instead of being taken up in this way “from outside (...) from perception”, they are to be “developed”, that is derived, through the unfolding of the simple determinations of an “economic cell-form” (23/12), namely of the commodity — this is the meaning of what Marx, as late as 1867, in a summary of some basic ideas of *Capital*, called his “dialectical method of development” (31/313). It is the same critique which Hegel in that § 231 of the *Encyclopaedia* also formulates by saying that the science “of the understanding” “proceeds from something *externally presupposed*”, must proceed from it, and that its axioms rest upon, and must rest upon, “the arbitrariness of choice”.[^8] This “*externally presupposed*” of the sciences of the understanding recurs with Marx in the concept of the “presuppositions of economics”, in which economic thinking is held captive, namely in its categories taken in from experience: “wages of labour, commerce, value, price (!), money etc.”. (2/33)

Georg Lukács was probably the first to recognize that for Marx “a dialectical derivation of the categories” is indispensable because in the “hierarchy” or “concrete totality of the categories”, thus in a “qualitative gradation”[^9] of lower and higher categories, “stages of actuality” have to be distinguished, so that Marx therefore “works with graded concepts of being”.[^10] That it is the economic things themselves (*Sachen*) which enforce a differentiation of the concept of existence is a position maintained later, presumably independently of Lukács, by Bruno Liebrucks; on the ground of his dialectical philosophy of language he formulated the thesis incompatible with Kantian philosophy and explicitly directed against it: “Exchange, the circulation of money and the circulation of capital have different concepts of existence as the basis of their objects.”[^11] On this example Liebrucks also demonstrates his Hegel-oriented critique of the traditional as well as Kantian dichotomization of existence and essence, which under the same aspect — namely in the context of a meta-critique of Kant's critique of the ontological proof of the existence of God — had already been relativized by Marx in his dissertation with regard to the existence of money.[^12]

In a manner similar to Liebrucks, Marx distinguishes — albeit already at the level of simple circulation — between “essential forms” and those which are “*devoid of content*, that is, no actual forms” (42/242). Instead of the “essential form” (23/565) we also hear of an “essential relationship” (23/564); and instead of an “irrational (*irrationell*)” or “imaginary” form Marx also speaks of a “semblance-form” (*Scheinform*) (34/151), which he defines in 1843 as a “form without content” — “a self-deceiving, (...) self-contradictory form”. (1/267)

Independently of the question whether the application of Hegelian categories — the concept of an “essential relationship”, for instance, is to be found in § 135 — and hence of the Hegelian critique “of the treatment by the understanding alone”[^13] of the social-scientific relationship of part and whole can be legitimated on the ground of the theory of science, what is in question in the first instance is the proof that and how content and form, object and method, material statements and categorical development — finally also object and subject — mutually permeate and condition one another. In diametrical contrast to the scientistic, model-theoretic procedure of economics we are here dealing not with hypotheses, basic assumptions, model-theoretic statements etc. pp., and in particular not with an object of knowledge fundamentally distinguished from the real object, but with the programme of a “genesis” of categories in their “real difference” (42/43) from one another and as “real” (42/159). The categories are no schemas and ideal types, but the expression of objective-subjective “forms of existence, determinations of existence” (42/40) of society.

Lukács sees the justification of this procedure in the fact that in economics, in diametrical contrast to “first” nature, we have to do not with objects given in advance but with a “self-created, ‘self-produced’ actuality”, with “a kind of second nature”[^14] — the “development” of those real categories is therefore conceived as their intellectual “production”, as their re-production. Its proper significance consists above all in the fact that it implies their *material* definition. Further advantages of this method could certainly be enumerated, but the question remains open with what right this procedure raises an exclusive claim to rationality. Its properties may recommend it as a correction or supplement of the procedure of normal science, but they do not by that alone ground its critique, and thereby the critique of normal-scientific economics as the quintessence of irrationality — evidently not for this reason alone, that Marxist economics itself evidently knows nothing whatever to do with that method of “development”. Incredible as it may seem: these problematics resulting from the Marxian concept of rationality, as also their first thematization by Lukács in 1923, were completely ignored by orthodox Marxism[^15] — not to mention academic economics.

Much the same happened at first to the treatise which, after *History and Class Consciousness*, is probably the most important contribution to the Marxian method: Max Horkheimer's *Zum Rationalismusstreit in der gegenwärtigen Philosophie*, which appeared in 1934 and which in the proper sense founds critical theory.[^16] For the first time in the reception-history of Marxian theory, the distinction between “mode of presentation” and “mode of research” (23/27) is here recognized in its constitutive significance for Marx's concept of social science.[^17] Although the *Grundrisse* and therewith the original shape of the Marxian method of development were still unknown at the time, Horkheimer was nevertheless able to characterize its intention as well as some of its basic features to perfection. The treatise contains almost all those basic ideas which were then, in the treatise written later, summed up under the title of “critical theory” and opposed to the academic philosophy and science characterized as ‘traditional theory’; their characteristic mark, Horkheimer sees, is that they are oriented to the rules of Descartes and to the dualism of being and thought underlying those rules: in the “traditional concept of theory”[^18] “subject and object fly apart”[^19] and the rules “of discursive or understanding-logic” hold.[^20]

This is not the place to report this critique in detail; instead it is to be sketched briefly and summed up only in so far as its relation to Hegelian and Marxian concept-formation is determined. Roughly sketched, there result two assignments which condition one another reciprocally: *on the one hand* that of understanding in the Hegelian, mode of research in the Marxian, and traditional theory in the Horkheimerian sense, and *on the other hand* that of reason in the Hegelian, mode of presentation in the Marxian, and critical theory in the Horkheimerian sense.

Horkheimer establishes that the principles “of ‘the logic of the understanding’ (...), the rules (...) of distinguishing thought” underlie that traditional logic of research which Marx named the “mode of research” and demonstrated on the example of Adam Smith: for Smith had merely described the “connexion appearing externally”, and hence subsumed it under “schematizing determinations of the concept”, that is, under “concepts of the understanding (!)”[^21] (26.2/162). This “mode of research” of not only Smith's but of economics as a whole is, according to Horkheimer too, quite unfitted for a “presentation of a concrete whole”, which means however: for the “reconstruction of living processes”,[^22] thus for the presentation of what Marx had called the “actual movement” (23/27), the real dynamics. The “fixed” concepts and “fixed”[^23] definitions of economics are therefore held to be fit only for the formation of static, not dynamic, theories of economic reality.[^24] The “actual movement” Marx calls the “life of the subject matter” (23/27), once also the “immanent soul of this subject matter (...), its right to a peculiar life”. (II.1/10) The subject matter is thus no dead, mechanical one, but rather one structured and moved within itself, and as such an adequate object of a “presentation” which looks, in the process, as though it were a “construction a priori”. (23/27)

When Adorno demands “concepts (...) which the thing (*Sache*) has from itself (...), that which the thing would want to be of its own accord”,[^25] and insists, on this point, that “empirical proofs (...) of central structural laws (...) always remain contestable”,[^26] then what is in question is nothing other than the Marxian problem of “presentation”: what matters to the latter is that which “bears on the principle”,[^27] and thus “holds the machinery together in secret”.[^28] Empirical “research”, by contrast, gains “at best classificatory superordinate concepts”, never such as would express the “life (!) of society”,[^29] — which is precisely what matters to “presentation”. What counted for Adorno as a prototypical shape of the “traditional theory” which negates the difference of essence and appearance was above all the methodology of Weber's essay on objectivity and the neo-Kantianism of Heinrich Rickert underlying it.[^30] Not only Comte and Mach, but also the representatives of the southwest-German idealism he subsumed under a broadly conceived and polemically used concept of “positivism”; and with Adorno this means by no means merely a philosophical position in the narrower sense, but always this position in the real connexion of a social-scientific methodology corresponding to it; the reformulation of the concept assigns to it a content which was later, in the ‘positivist dispute’, to be entirely ignored: the confusion of ‘first’ and ‘second nature’.

Only within the horizon of this problematics developed by Lukács does it become meaningful to characterize theorists as different as Rickert, Weber, Carnap, Popper and Parsons as “positivists”. Adorno's attack here aimed at the methodological credo of Max Weber: “Not the *‘thingly’* (*sachlichen*) connexions of *‘things’* (*Dinge*), but the *intellectual* connexions of *problems*, underlie the fields of work of the sciences”.[^31] In his confrontation with Weber's “bitter struggle” over the “apparently most elementary problems” of social economics, namely over “the manner of the formation of its concepts and their validity”,[^32] Adorno rejected above all the latter's thesis that what matters is to “unify” the social facts “into a self-consistent cosmos of *thought* connexions”.[^33] No doubt that Adorno agreed with Sombart's antithesis, that what matters is research into an existing, a “real connexion”, into that which “constitutes” the “reality” of “capitalism”, hence into what lies “behind the individual appearance”, “which no nominalism (...) can interpret away”.[^34]

This interpreting-away of essence, peculiar to “positivism”, is for Adorno only the consequence of his leveling of the categorical difference between ‘first’ and ‘second nature’, that is, between the object of natural science and that of social economics, between physical and socio-economic “objectivity” (*Gegenständlichkeit*). In the uniform hash of Rickert's “heterogeneous continuum”, that is, of the allegedly in nature as in society equally chaotic, unsurveyable, hence irrational multiplicity of things — a construction which follows from Rickert's “consciousness-philosophy developed to an extreme”[^35] — the “sensuous thing” is, however, set equal to the “sensuous supersensible” one.

As upon Sombart, Adorno too, in his critique of the “positivistic” neo-Kantianism of a Rickert and Weber, could have invoked Schumpeter, who, in manifest contradiction to his neo-positivist declamations, finally postulated that one must learn “to recognize the great unity (!) of economic appearances beneath the multiform surface (!) (...), (the) deeper truths lying beneath the surface”.[^36]

To the normal science of economics, a science of the understanding, there is thus denied, as a mere surface-science, the capacity for a cognition that would be even superficial, while the “deeper truths” of economics remain reserved for a privileged mode of cognition, namely for an “intuitive grasp”[^37], whereby he passes off such a “vision of things” as a “pre-analytical act of cognition”.[^38] The opposition of rationalism and irrationalism analysed by Horkheimer is evidently, for Schumpeter, of such a kind that they turn into one another. The “truths” gained irrationally are as such highly subjective and evade rational examination.

Adorno would have agreed with Schumpeter in that what matters is indeed the grasp of the “great unity”, that is, of the “principle” of society, and that in face of this “postulate of insight into the essence (!) of (...) society (...) the empirical contributions are drops on a hot stone”,[^39] because every “view of society as a whole (...) necessarily transcends its scattered facts”.[^40] He would however dispute that such a “reflection on what secretly holds sway”[^41] is referred to something like a “vision of things”, to irrational acts of a putative cognition; with Horkheimer he saw in irrationalism, rather and merely, the correlate which negates rationalism yet at the same time requires it, of one and the same anti-dialectical procedure of theory-formation. It lies on the surface, then, that there cannot be, for itself, a cognition of the “great unity” or of the “principle”, thus of the whole independently of its parts, and Adorno has therefore repeatedly pointed to the illusionariness of the representation which wants to devise ‘theories of the middle range’ instead of the ‘grand theory’. The whole is contained in the part, the macrocosm in the microcosm; and in the domain of sociological theory this can scarcely be contradicted.

If, for instance, Ulrich Beck wants to establish that there is a “capitalism without classes” and that the “marketing of risks by no means breaks with the developmental logic of capitalism”, but rather “lifts it to a new level”, then Jürgen Ritsert rightly criticizes Beck for making use of economic concepts “largely left in indeterminacy”.[^42] Who would doubt that the concepts of “capitalism” and of “developmental logic” can be legitimately used only within the framework of a “great” theory which bears on “the great unity”, on “the principle”; that only within its framework can the “indeterminacy” of the basic concepts be overcome by the development of material definitions?

Joseph Schumpeter too seems to know that beneath “the surface” is to be located not merely the “great unity”, but likewise the essential structure of such individual phenomena as capital and interest, value and money. Already in his early methodological writing he restricts his neo-positivist starting position to the effect that theories of distribution “do not rest immediately on observations of fact”, because in the case of interest, or of the question “whence it comes”, hence in the case of “theories of capital (...) we stand on the ground of theoretical construction and not on that of facts”; that “a connexion so hidden” — like the one between capital and interest — “does not result from observation”.[^43]

One may well call it a stroke of luck to come upon, in the economic literature, a treatise which frankly admits the precarious situation of economics with regard to its theories of distribution. It was the monetary theorist Hero Moeller who, with regard also to his own theoretical efforts, brings out that “the attempt to demonstrate the possibility of a doctrine so non-empirical (!) has to enter ground disputed to the utmost in methodological terms”.[^44]

Schumpeter did not use this now current term, yet in the case of the theories of distribution he described with precision the methodological problem of the subject matter entailed in such a “non-empirical doctrine”. But later too he was to remark, with regard to theories of capital, “that their material of facts is extremely scanty and in itself as a rule quite uninteresting”[^45] — uninteresting evidently in view of the fact that, in the case of the explanations of interest, what is at stake are the “real grounds (!) of the appearance”,[^46] not the “appearance” itself. With the concept of a “real ground” a metaphysical concept is introduced into theory, and at the same time the existence of a necessary connexion between this “ground” qua “essence” or “in itself” and the “appearance” is postulated. But how is such a necessity to be comprehended, when it can be neither a logical nor an empirical one subjected to the law of causality? Above all: how is one to represent to oneself the drawing-aside of the so-called “veil of money”, that is, of the empirical appearance?

Because in economics “every possibility of laboratory experiments” is lacking, because above all no electron microscope is able to lay open the “sources” of interest, that is, its “real ground”, there is in truth need of a “source of cognition” sui generis,[^47] whereby Schumpeter insists “that ‘explaining’ means something quite different (!) in the natural sciences than in the social sciences”.[^48] As little as present-day economics was he able to gain definitive clarity about the positive determination of this “whole other”. To be sure, what is at stake is also “the *meaning* of economic actions”, but this “explaining” is afflicted not merely by the fact that this meaning “is at the same time a source of controversies”,[^49] it also fails with regard to the determination of the “real ground” of interest and of other economic appearances. In fact, alongside this “source of cognition” and intuition Schumpeter had yet a third in view, related in intention to the Marxian and Horkheimerian “presentation”: a “conceptual reconstruction” of the appearances which he wanted to have strictly distinguished from the “historical genesis”. “We let (...) *become* what is in fact already present.”[^50] What is at stake, then, is an intellectual re-production of the thing.

With the principled distinction of such a “genesis” from the “historical genesis” Schumpeter must have thought of something like a “logical genesis” — an operation which is philosophical in itself. In fact, in the context of his discussion of theories of capital interest there is to be found the methodologically relevant thesis: “A truly ‘causal’ explanation would have to be ‘genetic’. It would have to lay bare the (logical) beginnings of things”,[^51] and he “demands (...) an explanatory *principle* which is not contained in the mere fact of the interdependence obtaining between all economic quantities”.[^52] Such a “principle” can be neither an axiom or a basic assumption nor a hypothesis. As a “logical beginning of things” it names, on the one hand, the “objective beginning” of the thing, its objective concept or “real ground”, which as the constituting factor must accrue to a mode of being other than that of the thing constituted — and, on the other hand, as “subjective beginning” it is to found a theoretical system; Marx's concept of social labour was conceived as such a subjective-objective principle.

Only on the assumption that the thing itself is conceptually mediated can one meaningfully speak of “logical beginnings of things” rather than of the beginning of a theoretical system. Schumpeter here breaks through the barriers of traditional theory marked out by the Cartesian dualism of thought and being, the hiatus irrationalis of model and reality enclosed within it. When he immediately adds that this may appear “ridiculous” “at first sight”, he concedes that such theories of capital interest are, under a “scientistic” aspect, of a “metaphysical” kind, hence “scientifically” undiscussable. But Schumpeter moves on forbidden ground not only in the field of the theory of capital, but also in those of the theories of money and of credit. Thus he contradicts the older theory of credit, whose position he characterizes with the sentence: “At the (logical) beginning stands money”,[^53] thus not credit, which is rather itself to be developed out of it, whereas Schumpeter conversely lets money “become” out of credit. Also in the case of the problems which belong to the theory of money in the narrower sense, what is at stake is the “logical” as opposed to the “historical (...) source of the money phenomenon”,[^54] the “logical source” in the sense of its “essence or its logic”;[^55] with regard to its purchasing power he likewise speaks here of the “logical source of exchange value”.[^56]

Only a single procedure is capable of fulfilling the sense of the concepts of a logical “becoming” which Schumpeter employed illegitimately: the Marxian “presentation” of the category ‘capital’. “We witness its process of creation. This dialectical process of creation is only the ideal expression of the actual movement in which capital becomes (!)”. (42/231)

When Hegel, in the Remark to § 3 of his *Philosophy of Right*, wants to have distinguished between an “external becoming” — namely the “historical exhibition and rendering-intelligible of the becoming (...) of the subject matter” — and its “development”, its “origin out of the concept”,[^57] what is at stake, in the proper sense, is the “becoming” of an institution or of a thing, at the same time the cognition of its “essence or its logic”. That is legitimate on the ground of an ontological logic which knows the opposition of subject and object behind it; and Schumpeter's “conceptual reconstruction” of a thing out of a “logical source” likewise cannot avoid laying down an objective concept of its logical genesis as the ground.

If one casts a glance at Schumpeter's own theory of money and at that of his contemporaries, their failure seems to demonstrate that as yet it lacks an adequate conceptuality. As then, so still today, holds good the sarcastic verdict of Georg Friedrich Knapp, who with regard to the task of “saying what money is” remarked: “This is what (...) economists ought to do, but are indeed not capable of doing it”.[^58] But the efforts of the Knapp school, too, and the discussions around Simmel's *The Philosophy of Money*,[^59] before and after the First World War, led scarcely a step further.

Like Knapp, Schumpeter too came in 1908 to the provocative thesis “that economics possesses neither a theory of money nor ever has possessed one”.[^60] He postulated — “here only in brief” — that in the “theoretical construction” of the “unity” of value, respectively of money, lies the “foundation stone of the theory of money” which is to be worked on in the first place.[^61] But soon he had to convince himself that the concept of money as “claim”, no less than that of “purchasing power”, harbours problems on account of the “indeterminacy of the object”, problems whose “depth” he wanted not even to have “indicated, let alone exhausted”;[^62] and he restricted his attempt to interpret “purchasing power” as an “appearance sui generis” (!!) — an “appearance” evidently not in the sense of the Kantian concept — to a presentation which knew how to circumscribe this mysterious “force” merely by means of an “analogy” and a “parable”.[^63]

In 1927 he took up his old themes again, reproached Gustav Cassel with “relinquishing every clarification of the problematics of the unit of account”,[^64] — “there is no trace of any derivation or justification”[^65] — and began working out a manuscript on money and currency; in 1929 he had its publication announced, in 1939 a second time, but it was never to appear. In the manuscript the editor found “frequently occurring (...) sighs: ‘Why’ or ‘How so?’”[^66] As in the *Money Manuscript*, and presumably as an echo of it, so in a chapter on money of the *History of Economic Analysis* there is likewise to be found a resigning sigh: “It is an indisputable fact that views on money are as difficult to describe as drifting clouds.”[^67]

If Schumpeter answers the economic Gretchen question, “Is economic theory a science at all?”[^68] rather evasively — “We have not yet got over the mountain, indeed we are a long way from being on it”[^69] — then what will have stood before his eyes above all are the possibilities of grounding non-empirical procedures in the theory of capital and the theory of money; but not those alone. No less problematic did macroeconomics appear to him. “Strictly speaking”, its aggregates and basic concepts figure for him as “meaningless”, concepts such as aggregate demand and aggregate supply “have (...) no meaning”.[^70] He therefore mocks the fact that the founders of macroeconomics — he names among others Keynes — operate with these aggregates “without (...) symptoms of a critical misgiving”.[^71] The suspicion presses itself upon one that the late Schumpeter, in view of his helplessness as to how the macro-aggregates used by himself were to be “saved”, felt prompted and challenged to turn once again to the study of the Marxian theory of value. Evidently what was at stake for him here was no longer, as formerly, problems of price theory, hence no longer “relative value”, but “absolute” value, and this quite evidently with a view to his attempt at a determination of the meaning of the macroeconomic aggregates, that is, under the aspect of the conditions of their addability and their intertemporal existence — seen methodologically: what is usually conceived as the “salvation of the phenomena”,[^72] here of the macroeconomic ones —, their salvation from being declared “meaningless”. Only under this aspect does he succeed in grasping the “absolute value” as the “central concept” of the Marxian theory of value;[^73] he now sees through, self-critically, the “error” of his own critique of Marx, “which points to a misunderstanding of the essential (!) point of the Marxian theory of value”.[^74]

Schumpeter's talk of the “meaningless” concepts of macroeconomics is reminiscent of Joan Robinson's thesis that money, the rate of interest, commodities and purchasing power — elsewhere she names capital and national income too — turn out to be “ungraspable concepts if we really try to hold on to them”.[^75] It strikes the eye that both of them, Schumpeter and Robinson, do not declare one central concept of economics meaningless and ungraspable: “price”.

In truth: the concept of price — more closely, the formalizable relative price as “pivot and hinge”[^76] of marginalist analysis — seems to both theorists the absolute and indubitable foundation, the safe anchorage in the sea of economic uncertainties. As is well known, Schumpeter rhapsodizes over the Walrasian price theory as a “Magna Carta of economic theory”,[^77] over Walras as that “greatest of all economists” who led economics “to the heights of a firmly grounded, exact science”[^78] — even though, on closer inspection, it turns out to be merely a “building site”[^79] and is “in the last resort perhaps nothing” other than a “vast research programme”.[^80] And when Robinson and the neo-Ricardianism assert its failure and instead proclaim the price system of Piero Sraffa to be the “Magna Carta of economic theory”, there are in turn good reasons for being able to demonstrate the “fiasco” of this “research programme” too and “the disaster of neo-Ricardian theory”.[^81]

By no means have all economists understood and accepted the price as an unquestionable presupposition of economic theory-formation. Thus Alfred Amonn registered a deficit of the mainstream of economics with his finding: “It does not ask after how the price-form arises; instead it simply presupposes the price-form as given, and in the doctrine of method we determine what presupposition this price-form has. The problem of economics is the content of price, which is a determinate quantity”.[^82]

It was of course not this thesis to which Amonn's early methodological work owed its repute as an “epoch-making work”[^83]; rather, it was his dogmatic reception of the “newer logic” and doctrine of method of Rickert and Weber, including their uncritical application to economics; Rickert's “heterogeneous continuum”, thus the allegedly unstructured and chaotically given material, the “thingly object” or the “thingly empirical domain”, he names the “object of experience”, which “on account of the principially infinite multiplicity of its features cannot be structured conceptually”; over against this irrational material he sets the material first ordered by the economist under the “social point of view”, as the “object of knowledge” now constituted. This dichotomization of the object of economics was already “rather generally accepted”[^84] in 1932 and had become “a matter of course”[^85] not only for the economics of his time, but even today a representative of “critical realism” comprehends Amonn's distinction as “purposeful”.[^86]

Just as Adorno sought to prove that Weber's material investigations belie his methodological self-understanding, so it will emerge here too that the insights Amonn gained on the material contradict his proclaimed methodology, but also his ultimately subjectivist theory of value, so that recourse had to be had to all manner of vaguenesses. Thus with his thematization of price as form, that is, of the “*‘social ropes’* or *‘form’*”[^87] of economic facts, he had on the one hand “moved away from the marginal utility school”,[^88] and was also compelled to move away from it; and because he nevertheless sought to hold fast to it, his “doctrine of method” offers, in place of an explanation, scarcely more than a description of that paradoxical structure of the socio-economic categories.

Evidently what shocked Amonn was his discovery that with price a social “*homogeneity*” of the objects is found, one which “is no intuitable, no real” one in the sense of an empirical mode of observation, but “*accessible to thought alone* (...), that is, exists only in the thoughts of human beings”; at the same time this homogeneity must “*figure for all* (...) in the same manner”. The “price is *not* a *quantity of real goods*”, but a number “*ideal*” in character, in the consciousness of living units; it is “*no real, sensuously perceptible* phenomenon”, but a something which, “in the consciousness of all”[^89], exists. He also calls it the “general consciousness” of the “validity” of an “ideal and socially valid unit”; in the “*general* consciousness” of the subjects of exchange there exists an “ideal, mental unit given in the same way, as measure of prices or *unit of exchange value*”.[^90]

Amonn did not remain the only economist to insist that “*the price itself is problematic*” — price as such — and to criticize the mathematized, functional theories of price because they nonetheless “presuppose price as *given*”.[^91] Thus the Casselian theory of prices, a simplified shape of the Walrasian one, was charged with “having as its presupposition an existence of prices pure and simple”, and with never asking “*why* price systems are (...), *why a price system is possible at all*”; what is in question here are “unreal magnitudes”, of which “not a single one stands in a causal relation to another”, and which therefore “are not realities, but concepts”.[^92] And the Walrasian theory of prices was criticized by the neo-Austrian school equally under the aspect that its models, aiming at the calculation of “*purely quantitative* relations” between economic magnitudes, proceed from relations already given, “*already finished*, that is, come about one knows not how”, and thus from “the movements of a market as existing and given”, and likewise from the “givenness of a unit of account” which is supposed to exist as money; what slips in here is the “*vicious circle*” that “the market validity of this unit of account is already the *consequence* of price”,[^93] which is itself only first to be calculated. It goes without saying that these general objections apply also to the functional theory of prices of neo-Ricardianism.

Amonn's assumption of a “general consciousness”, in the sense of a supraindividual entity as the condition of existence for the being of “abstract”, “ideal” and universally valid units — an assumption which strikes us today as mystifying — is likewise found in a whole series of esteemed economists: in Sombart, as we saw, but above all in Ernst Wagemann, for whom the “processes of the money-essence” lie in a “collective *substance*”, in a “collective understanding”: “The absolute value of money is an appearance of the collective consciousness, and it is by no means willing to vacate its place to scientific insight.”[^94] And if this renowned theorist of money and of the business cycle sees in “value” no less than in “price” an “appearance which is always merely collective”, a “manifestation-form of the collective”,[^95] then these phenomena too, and in particular “absolute” price — is it not determined by the “absolute” value of money which “springs” from the collective consciousness[^96] — seem to him to close themselves off against “scientific insight”.

For Wagemann and Amonn no less than for Gottl-Ottlilienfeld do price and the “ideal measure of prices”, respectively the money which is the “means of expressing prices”, “stand in a relationship of mutual logical conditioning, of a necessary correlation”, such that with the one the other “necessarily”[^97] arises and persists. This critic of the ruling economics stresses emphatically that his position “crosses” that of Amonn “in the clearest and most decided manner”.[^98] In academic economics it was probably Gottl who first recognized the proper problematics of this correlation; for he describes it as an exceedingly paradoxical one, which confronts the economist with a task hitherto unsolved and, within economics itself, scarcely solvable: a “squaring of the economic circle”:[^99] the commodity refers to money and vice versa, so that the solution of the problem of money presupposes that of the value-price problem. And vice versa: the separation of the so-called veil of money, and therewith also the splitting apart of price into “relative” and “absolute” components, thus the dichotomization of the economic world into a “real” and a monetary sector, prove themselves on this account to be operations logically deeply questionable — since the “reality” of price, and of money too, consists in just this real relationship of mutual conditioning, which can be neither examined nor derived by the formalized theory of relative price, nor by the formalized theories of money, and so must always already have been presupposed.

Under a methodological aspect too, the commodity-money correlation turns out to be a phenomenon highly paradoxical for the ruling doctrine: we are dealing, after all, with an objective and “real relation”[^100] between things and not with a merely thought one — that is, with a fetishistic relationship whose existence collides not only with certain existential presuppositions of the subjective theory of value but, far beyond that, also with those of all epistemologies oriented or inspired in a subjectivist, hence Kantian manner, whereby, contrary to Amonn's thesis, the distinction of the object of experience, respectively of the real object, from the object of knowledge has also become void, at least in the domain of socio-economic theories.

The whole of what one, neatly separated from each other, has usually called price, objective exchange value/value, and “abstract”/“ideal” unit of account or unit of value — this necessary connexion of those moments of the correlation Gottl characterizes as a particular dimension of things, unknown to the natural sciences, and names it the “economic dimension”: a fourth dimension, as it were, hence one which exists neither physically nor, indeed, in the sense of the philosophy of reflection. He arrives at the provocative thesis that the structure of this whole no less than the peculiarity of its single moments has proved itself unobservable to all authors and schools of economics at enmity with one another: “The connexions which weave themselves through actuality regardless of the Economic dimension defy being unravelled; one cannot unfold them clearly, because thought is for ever caught up in them.”[^101] For Gottl it is the thinking of the discipline of economics which, above all in the teaching on value and price, is for ever “caught up” in contradictions and paradoxes, which has thought the “state of affairs” and the “expressions ‘price’ and ‘exchange value’” to shame, and likewise the concept of “capital”; all three concepts “are stiff with intellectual impurity”.[^102]

It is no different with the other “basic concepts” such as “good”, “national income”, “national wealth”, “production”, “productive”/“unproductive”, “capitalism” etc. pp. Without exception all these categories turn out, for this critic of economics, to be merely “words standing in for a problem”. If Gottl-Ottlilienfeld goes on to assert that economics is lacking a fundamental question, a “*fundamental problem*”[^103], and that accordingly no clarification of the basic concepts is in sight at all, one will immediately recall the critique of Schumpeter and of Robinson, who decades later were to come to the same result (...) But what presses itself upon one is the thesis of Hegel/Marx discussed at the outset: that what is in question in the first instance is not at all the thinking of the discipline of economics as such, which is “for ever caught up” and must indeed become so, but this thinking in its peculiarity as merely “understanding” thinking.

The problems described by Gottl in the context of his discussion of the “economic circle” were by no means unknown; but within academic discourse they were thematized not by economists, rather by a philosopher, by Georg Simmel. A convincing solution was, to be sure, denied him. On the reasons he expressed himself, to the value philosopher Heinrich Rickert, in the words that he “groans and despairs (...) over certain paradoxes in the relationship of value and money. It is precisely the most elementary thing (!) which has so far presented me with insuperable difficulties”;[^104] this “despair” over the aporetic structure of the apparently simple, familiar, “most elementary thing” finally drove him to renounce a socio-economic solution of the value-money problem in favour of metaphysical postulates of value; and to this metaphysical semblance-solution it is probably primarily to be ascribed that his philosophy of money, and with it, regrettably, his acute sketch of the problem, remained almost unnoticed in economics.[^105]

Gottl's “Economic Dimension”, that state of affairs which according to him “is stiff with problems”, and which he evidently wanted to be understood as the proper, hitherto misrecognized fundamental problem of economics, is not exhausted in the aporia of the “economic circle”; rather what matters to him is that it is this dimension which first imparts to the economic objects their characteristic stamp, which constitutes them as economic ones: “To all these objects there pertains, somehow, a length, or a weight, or a temperature, or an age. Now, in no way other than these is there affixed to these objects (...) a *characteristic number* of the sense of a *magnitude which figures*.”[^106] In a lengthy treatise on the problematics of Gottl it is above all that “affixing” of the number to the objects which occupies Amonn — that it “in no way other” than physical properties “is affixed” — and his question, more than once repeated, reads: “Truly: *‘in no way other’?*” What is in question is “price”, which is to belong to the things somehow as a property, which is to “be affixed” to them. We are standing in the middle of Marx's doctrine of the “fetish character of the commodity and its secret”. The most elementary thing, price, turns out to be a “mystical” phenomenon (23/85), the commodity therefore a “hieroglyphic” (23/88) and a “deranged form” (23/90), because this property is a logically impossible one: a “social natural property of these things” (23/86), that is, a “supernatural property” (23/71) of natural things. We find ourselves, however, in a certain way also in the midst of those “subtleties” (23/12) of *price*-form analysis, which had evidently pressed themselves upon those non-Marxian economists from the matter itself.

Amonn does not deny outright that price affixes itself as a “property”; but because what is in question are properties sui generis, he enters this caveat: “Weight, length, temperature, age are (...) all objective qualities adhering to the things themselves and externally perceptible. Of the state of affairs which Gottl calls the ‘Economic Dimension’ this is by no means so unconditionally true.”[^107] Gottl was entirely conscious of the difference between the “adhering” of physical and that of economic properties; his more correct formulation of the problem, namely, ran: with the number as a “named number”, thus “in marks, francs etc.”, there is “accorded to every unit of a type of goods a like (!) extension (...) as, for instance (!), to a corporeal thing its length, its weight”.[^108] And that, in the case of the “Economic Dimension”, what is in question is no “externally perceptible quality” is not merely conscious to Gottl — he also designates the extension as “transdimensional”, and accordingly comprehends it, in the sense of the Marxian characterization, as a “supersensible” dimension — but rather he will have seen its “tremendous problematics”[^109] precisely here: namely that of a quality not externally perceptible. For the question does press itself upon one here: how can such a “quality”, how can a supernatural, because social, property nevertheless be “perceived”? But above all, the “tremendous problematics” of the dimension is what Gottl will have seen in this: how is one to think that “affixing” or “adhering” of those supersensible “qualities” to physical, sensible things?

Heidegger had this problematics, or at any rate a kindred one, before his eyes, in a more general formulation, when he made the concept of value of the value philosophers[^110] Lotze and Rickert into a problem; on this point it is to be borne in mind that Rickert at times, in the sense of Simmel, subsumed also the economic concept of value under this concept of value: “What does value signify ontologically? How is this ‘adhering’ and being-burdened to be grasped categorially? (...) Darkness of this structure of a being-burdened-with-value (...). What, then, does the being of the values, or their ‘validity’, signify ontologically (...) What does this ‘adhering’ of the values to the things mean ontologically? (...) These determinations (remain) in the dark”.[^111] How is it with Simmel's philosophy of value, does Heidegger's ironic turn strike him too, that he lets things be “stuck with” a value?[^112] Above all, how is it with economics? Is it the case that it “sticks” things with a value and, generically, with economic predicates, just as one sticks a stamp on a letter?

Now Carl Menger had already grounded the subjective theory of value with the argument that value can be “nothing adhering to goods”, for it is “a judgment which (...) does not exist outside consciousness (...) For that which subsists objectively is, after all, always only the things.”[^113] Menger advances here acute and hard-to-refute arguments in ground of the subjective theory of value, yet he himself has to concede that value “steps towards us”; but only something objective in some way is capable of “stepping towards” us. It is only a “meaning”, to be sure one which we somehow “transfer onto the goods”; but if it at the same time “steps towards” us, such a “transfer” can at best be understood as the doing of a “general consciousness”.

Alongside these paradoxies, a further consequence followed from subjectivism, one worked out by Menger's pupil Wieser, namely that even “exchange value in the national-economic (objective) sense” does not exist at all: “*In truth there is* no ‘objective’ exchange value at all (...) (it) is the concordant section of the valuations (...) *the concord of individual valuations*.”[^114] What we have here are highly speculative assumptions, wholly unfitted to derive the opposite of that which “*there is in truth*”, namely the “objective” or necessary *semblance*, the semblance[^115] of an exchange value in the national-economic sense which “steps towards” us as something objective. Even the other pupil of Menger, Böhm-Bawerk, was unable to follow that radical subjectivism; for he defines objective exchange value — “the value phenomenon of national and social economics par excellence” — as a “capacity of a good”, namely as its “*power in exchange*”, which “is to be attributed to goods as a property”,[^116] hence as something objective in some way, something adhering to them. The so-called valuations of the subjects may concern their magnitude, but not the bare existence of this property which “affixes itself”. If Joan Robinson, for instance, formulates that every “exchangeable commodity represents at the same time itself and a definite quantity of purchasing power”,[^117] then this abstract “force”, or “quantity” of force, affixed to a good, which can never at all be derived from estimations of value, cannot be distinguished from the “power in exchange” of Böhm-Bawerk, from his “objective exchange value”.

The young Schumpeter followed his teacher Wieser in this, that there can be no more any “‘objective’ value”: “It *only seems* (!) to be such a one”;[^118] later the objectivity of this semblance pressed itself upon him so massively that, unconcerned about his earlier subjectivism, he was to make use, now and then, of a quite fetishistic way of speaking: he now knows a “transfer of value” which brings it about “that nowhere along the way does a piece (!) of value remain stuck (!)”, there are “quantities of value” which “affix”(!) themselves to products and which “adhere” (!) to them.[^119] Evidently Böhm-Bawerk and Schumpeter, like all economists who, in screaming contradiction to their subjective theory of value and of price, nonetheless pay homage to a macroeconomic objectivism, had not understood Menger's principled objection to “objective” or macroeconomic value at all; Wieser gave it a clearly delineated and at the same time a vivid formulation: since this value “is not supposed to have reality as a subjective appearance and yet cannot have reality as an appearance of nature, (it is) in contradiction with all that we are accustomed to think about the things between heaven and earth”.[^120]

Wieser draws from this the challenging consequence that macroeconomic or “objective” value is “meaningless” (!); to this explanation, however, he adds a qualification, acutely and vividly formulated: it is meaningless, unless we were to “assume a new (!) order between (!) the physical and the psychical appearances. (...) If this is not the case, then it is impossible for us to represent it without contradiction with the rest of our thinking.”[^121] As we shall see, Menger himself is ultimately irresolute, yet Wieser, and with him the mainstream of economics, remain entrenched in the deeply rooted, mostly unreflected conviction that one is simply not able to represent, beyond this dualism of the physical and the psychical — akin to the classical dualism of thought and being, and where the psychical of value-*feeling* leads to a value-*judgment*, hence to an act of thought — a third, a “new order”. This was soon to prove an error, for in the footsteps of Bolzano and Lotze, Simmel and Frege postulated the “existence” of such an intermediate realm, of a “third realm”[^122] — though the Simmelian *Philosophy of Money* was indeed not such as to vindicate this philosopheme in the domain of social philosophy. Nevertheless, a shared problematics between Marx and Simmel emerges, in the negative, beyond doubt: the logical place of value and money is to be located only “beyond (...) objectively-external or subjectively-psychical existence (...) beyond subjective being (...) but also beyond objective being”, precisely in a “third realm (...) beyond (!) subject and object”.[^123]

What is at stake here is not merely the thinkability of the Marxist and, far beyond that, precisely of the macroeconomic objectivism too — in the sense of a consistent subjectivism Robert Liefmann insisted relentlessly upon this anti-macroeconomic consequence — but possibly the thinkability of the theory of price itself, and therewith of the object of economics at all: at any rate if one follows the majority opinion of economics, which takes up the position, like Simmel, that “price, according to its conceptual essence, coincides with economically objective value”.[^124] This is not the place to pursue this thesis which has remained controversial; yet its plausibility lies on the surface: if I grasp price as a quantity of “purchasing” or, better, of “exchange power”, then I can scarcely avoid the paradoxical assertion that price, qua value, in some way “adheres” to the things and even “sticks” (Heidegger) to them like chewing gum to the teeth. The argument of Menger/Wieser could turn out to be a boomerang, namely the precise index of the unthinkability of the subjective teaching of value and of price as well.

Simmel seems to have been perfectly clear that his problematics concern the conditions of the possibility of a science of economics as such. Just as Marx, and later Amonn and Gottl too, had understood price as an unreflected and quasi-apriori “presupposition” of economics, so Simmel too states: “In the empirical (!) economy the things are wont (...) to have long been provided with the value-sign by the time they enter into exchange.”[^125] Always already, in quasi-apriori fashion, a price, namely a value-sign, “adheres” to them; the economic things confront the theorist of price as always already constituted ones. Comparable to Schumpeter, only more reflective, Simmel here analyses on the “level of the thingly-logical, not of the historically-genetic, understanding” (ibid.); and similarly to Amonn, only more reflective, he asks here not after the quantitative determining grounds of price, but after the conditions of its existence.

Like Simmel, Amonn too affirms the possibility of a “new order” of things; and, unlike Wieser, he sees it long since realized in the order of economic things: “The concept of reality has, precisely, in the social sciences a quite different (!) sense than in the natural sciences. In the latter only what is sensuously experienced figures as reality, whereas the social sciences have to do with an object which is itself, on the whole, not (...) sensuously experienceable (...), merely *graspable by thought*.”[^126] Amonn arrives here at a finding which, for a Kantian, is certainly striking: that in economics we do have to do with “very real things”, although “they are not graspable by hand and can be neither seen with the eyes nor heard with the ears”.[^127] It is above all a daring thesis, to assert the existence of paradoxical entities of this kind, because the question immediately forces itself upon one: are we dealing here with an “object of experience”, or with its opposite, with an “object of knowledge”? And one will go on asking what, here, constitutes the objectivity of the object, if it is evidently not to be that of a physical object?

Whereas Amonn was able to wrest this remarkable insight into economics as a reality sui generis only in contradiction to his own methodological intentions — and, as will emerge, he was to, and had to, forget it again soon —, Simmel's desperate efforts are devoted to the attempt, which ultimately failed him, to work out the philosophical concept of this new order of things which the macroeconomists affirm without reflection but deny methodologically.

Simmel certainly agrees with Menger and Wieser that value is no “property” but “a judgment remaining in the subject”, yet, in contradiction to these strict subjectivists, he proceeds from the assumption that it does nevertheless, in a certain way, “adhere” to the object; the object “possesses” the “form” of value qua “demand or claim”, it “*demands* (...) to be acknowledged”;[^128] consciousness “finds it, as a fact”. Thus there “remains to the subjectivity of value” only the “negative” meaning: “that value does not adhere to the objects themselves in the same sense as colour or temperature”; “neither the deeper sense and content of the concept of value, nor (...) the practical-social formations linked to it (...) are adequately comprehended in any way by its assignment to the ‘subject’”; “subjectivity” is “something merely provisional and in fact not very essential”,[^129] whereby Simmel has denied the claim to validity of the subjective theory of value. Under the aspect of the objective, Simmel formulates this thought also as follows: “even if the value of an object is not objective in the same sense as its colour or its weight, it is for all that by no means subjective in the sense corresponding to *this* objectivity (...). The practical relationship to things (...) generates a quite other kind of objectivity”, an “economic objectivity”.[^130]

What matters to him is precisely that paradoxie which Menger had indeed seen but not grasped: that in fact something like an “objectification of value”[^131] takes place — “the subjective processes (...) objectify themselves in value”, “the content of will and feeling receives the form of the object”;[^132] in this way there stands before the subjective theory of value the task, unsolvable within economics, of explaining the “objectivation of representations”, their “setting forth into an existence-form standing over against us”,[^133] with the result that we find ourselves facing a “realm of values”, an “objective realm” — Marx's “objective world of values” (42/368) — namely the homogeneous social product, as well as the quantity of money and its movement. The “objectivity” of value, and therewith its thingly character, namely its “adhering” to a thing, is merely the reverse side of the indubitable “going-beyond-the-single-subject of its validity”,[^134] thus of the indubitable fact which first constitutes economics as *social economics*: that “value is suprasubjective, supra-individual”.[^135]

And just as Marx does, Simmel recognizes that the problem of the super-individual, and precisely for that reason “*super*sensible”, mode of being of the magnitudes of economics — their being as “social natural properties” or “*super*natural properties” of natural things — is solvable only in its entwinement with another problem, that of the “objective being-for-one-another”[^136] of things, of their “being grown into one another”,[^137] such that “out of the alongside-one-another of things a with- and for-one-another” has become;[^138] this relation which Simmel calls “value-form” (!)[^139] — a “relativity become substance” (!)[^140] or a “suprapersonal relationship between objects”[^141] — resembles, structurally, Marx's “value-form”, which is well known to have been defined as a “social relationship of objects” (23/86), and more precisely as a “relationship of things among themselves” (26.3/127), which constitutes an objective, self-relating and real relation, thus a relation impossible not merely for the Kantian.[^142]

How little the problematics of “economic objectivity” had been understood by Wieser's pupil Schumpeter is shown by his polemic against the allegedly “senseless controversy” between “subjective” and “objective” theories of value and of price; Schumpeter's grotesque misunderstanding repeated itself in the polemics of present-day Walrasians against the Marxian labour theory of value. He appealed to the fact that the “subjective” theory “must always fall back on ‘objective’ facts (data)”, which is why every “subjective theory is also objective”;[^143] in the period before his self-critical revision of the quantitative misinterpretation of the Marxian labour theory of value — this occurred only in the last years of his life — he inferred from this that the latter too, like every “objective” theory of value, is to be treated as a special case of the Walrasian theory of prices. At the bottom of this misjudgement lies, in the last resort, a category mistake: the identification of “objective” in the sense of “second” with “first nature”, the inability even to bring the peculiar mode of being of the economic object into view.

Schumpeter and his modern adherents forget that it was precisely Wieser who had opposed the possible existence of the “objective value” of the labour theory of value — a possibility he denied as unthinkable — to the “physical appearances”, hence also to the Walrasian “data”: physical quantities and technical relations. And Simmel had specified this problem to the effect that the “objectivity” of the value of the thing “is not objective in the same sense as its colour”; if Amonn now delimits economic “reality” from that of natural science by saying that it “can be neither seen with the eyes nor heard with the ears”, then he too disputes setting the objectivity of “objective value” equal to the objectivity of physical things, respectively data; the problem raised by Menger and Wieser consists precisely in this, that an “objectivity” is supposed to obtain here which is sensuously not perceptible — Marx's “ghostly” or “abstract value-objectivity”.

What with Simmel and other economists is called “*objectivation*”, Marx designates as “*objectification*” — occasionally he uses both terms synonymously.[^144] And it is primarily this supra-individual process, which unfolds itself “behind the back” of the individuals, to which Marx's proper interest is directed from the outset: “The objectification of the general, social character of labour (and therefore (!!) of the labour-time contained in exchange value) makes their product precisely into exchange value”. (42/101) “The relations of the private workers to the social total labour are *objectified* over against them (!)”. (II.5/47)

As one sees, what matters to Marx in the first instance is the genesis of the economic object, its “objectivity”, its constitution; and only for this reason, that labour with its “general” properties can be apprehended as a suitable constituting element, does it also come into question as immanent measure and regulator of the quantitative relations of exchange; the problem of determining that which academic economics calls relative prices is, for Marx, of secondary nature. What now figures as priority is that question which has remained a taboo for economics: the question after the “objectivity” of price, after the circumstance that it, as a “social natural property” of a thing, “stands over against” us.

This objectivity of the commodity, whose “ghostly *value-objectivity* is (...) not perceptible” (II.6/7), is therefore an “abstract objectivity”. (II.6/12) Herewith that main problem of meta-economic concept-formation is given which in the academic literature was first recognized by Simmel and designated as the problem of the “real abstraction”. What is in question, then, is not a problem produced by the labour theory of value; it poses itself rather, and precisely, also for those who, like Simmel, proceeded from subjective value: “the objective (!) process, which often enough also governs the consciousness of the single individual, abstracts, so to speak, from the fact that it is values which form its material, and wins its ownmost essence in the *equality* of the same (...), so that not only the contemplation of the economy, but the economy itself consists, so to speak, in a real abstraction (...)”.[^145]

Although Marx on one occasion even uses the same term — the “abstraction” which “is performed daily” is a “real” one, and it “*exists*” in “average labour” (13/18) — and although with him there accrues to “capital in general”, to an “abstraction (...), a *real* existence” (42/362), not merely academic economics but Marxist economics too has preferred to keep silent about this. The first work in which that “real” and “existing abstraction” was recognized as the proper central problem of the Marxian analysis of fetishism, that is, of the analysis of the “sensuous supersensible thing”, appeared to be sure almost fifty years after *Capital*, and remained wholly unregarded in the academic as in the Marxist literature;[^146] only with Adorno again — prompted by Sohn-Rethel — does the problem of the “real abstraction”, still in economics without exception under a taboo, win a systematic place.

What economics does not know, that it does unconsciously, and occasionally it even gives it utterance. If the commodity, for instance, is grasped by Böhm-Bawerk as a “power in exchange”, by Robinson as a “quantity of purchasing power”, and the commodity qua capital by Amonn even as the representative of an “abstract power of disposal”,[^147] then what correlates with these concepts is a “real abstraction” from use values; for these “powers” and “forces”, and above all the “quantities” of them, are understood as homogeneous, and thus “abstract”, indifferent to whether this attribute is employed or not. With the express characterization of the “objects as abstract magnitudes”[^148] there has merely been formulated, though not thought, what is unconsciously intended by those other concepts — better: analogies, parables, “words standing in for a problem”: the fourth, the “Economic Dimension”.

The description of its most important peculiarity, as a “dimension” so obscure, brings Gottl now into the most intimate proximity to that concept which he, like Simmel and Amonn, seeks to evade even at the price of grotesque misinterpretations of the labour theory of value: Marx's “value as such” in the sense of a “qualitative equality” (23/76) or of an “abstract objectivity” of commodities, then: value not as centre of gravity of prices — only in this reduced shape does academic economics, and in particular neo-Ricardianism, know it — but as “Economic Dimension” of the economic magnitude, which proves itself also, and precisely, as the quintessence of objective equivalence: “Equivalence, as, so to speak, a *paradoxical* equality; things *are* indeed different from one another, but in a determinate relation (...) they are nevertheless *held* to be equal (...) a paradoxical equality *wrenched* from inequality (...) equality despite thingly inequality (...) equivalence (...) as a capacity to be set equal despite difference”.[^149] The “paradoxical equality”, or Amonn's “objective homogeneity”, is the meta-economic condition of the possibility not merely of scaling economic magnitudes, but of adding them as well, and of establishing, at the macroeconomic level, equations between monetary and real quantities. A further peculiarity of the fourth dimension, described by Simmel too, demonstrates its intimate kinship also with the Marxian value-*form*: “relation of *‘reciprocal worth’* (...), in that (!) each one (!) refers itself to the other as its ‘value’. But to ‘equal worth’, to equivalence, there belongs the representation of that which is *the same*, contained in both sets of objects (...) power of disposal”.[^150]

Whereas Marx's analysis of form, following Hegel's analysis of the concept of force above all in the third chapter of the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, undertakes the attempt to make the economic *force*-concept more precise — by means of the category of “doubling” —, there is to be found, no less than in Amonn, not even in the remotest approach, in Gottl's investigation either, an attempt to develop conceptually that self-relating and real relation, as well as this “word standing in for a problem” of a social “power”[^151] or “force” which “adheres” to a thing. This problem of “adhering” can also be expressed thus: how is one to think the containment, the “presence”, of the one, the same, the homogeneous and the abstract in the other, namely in the many, the different, the heterogeneous and the concrete? Evidently we are dealing here already, thus in the sphere of commodities, with a definite modification of the problem of universals;[^152] a problem already of the theory of value, not only of the theory of money. This has been overlooked by those few authors whose rank nevertheless consists in their having recognized, at all, its relevance for economics; to be sure, they immediately restricted it to the monetary and misrecognized its universal significance. Thus in Simmel: money “is the particular realization of that which the objects have in common as economic objects (...), universale ante rem as well as in re as well as post rem (...), it is the graspability of the most abstract, whose meaning lies most of all in super-particularity”.[^153]

Gottl has formulated something similar, but the most important question seems to me to consist in this, whether the problem of universals is to be located primarily on the level of the theory of value or not. For it must be considered that presumably only on the basis of the labour-theory-of-value synthesis of particular, concrete and general, abstract labour is it meaningful — namely, groundable in a theory of society and not merely metaphysically — to speak of a “universale in (!) re”, of a universal “in” the particular, respectively the single. In the socio-economic sphere we are by no means dealing with those classical problems of the Platonic *chorismos*, with that space-time “apartness of sensible thing and supersensible idea”,[^154] what is in question is not the “difference of essentia and res”,[^155] the “intelligibile in sensibili”,[^156] or, conversely, the thing “as a manifestation of incarnate ideas”.[^157]

What is in question is not at all use value as a “sensible thing”, which is to be conceived as a “copy” of an idea, of a “supersensible”, in such a way that it is constituted by this “supersensible” in its knowability and in its being. Use value by no means “participates” in value as an eternal, otherworldly, perhaps divine “archetype”. If the commodity is consumed qua sensible thing, its “supersensible” disappears likewise. Because the latter is dependent on use value, so that the “supersensible” in the economic sphere is dependent on the sensible, the economic shape of the “supersensible” can by no means be set equal to an ontological one, nor can the value of the commodity by any means be understood as a Platonic idea.

Nevertheless, the socio-economic transformation of the problem of universals is by no means a merely bizarre notion of a philosopher; thus the renowned theorist of money Eduard Lukas too establishes the significance of the problem of universals for the theory of money: “In the money-sign (...) — (...) paradoxical as it may sound — *abstract economic value* is brought to real appearance. And money ‘as money’ *is* now such economic value pure and simple: whereas otherwise the (...) value of a good of use, as a *concrete* economic value, appears to attach to the presupposition of its (...) use value (...) and to be bound to the latter”. And in the footnote belonging to this he indicates the philosophical implication of this finding of the theory of money in a manner which agrees entirely with Simmel's philosophy of money: “A matter for reflection for many who (...) would concede to the ‘abstract’ no kind of existence whatever”.[^158]

The “new order” of things rejected by Wieser, which stands in contradiction to everything we are “accustomed to think”, is therefore long since present, in the shape of money, as an abstract which is not merely thought but also exists: a “universale in (!) re”. Once again we pose Amonn's question after that socio-economic “reality” which is supposed to have a “quite other sense” than the one ascertained by natural science. Is money an “object of experience” or an “object of knowledge”? And this question now sharpens to this: that, with Simmel, Amonn, Max Weber and Rickert, one has to ask: what is to be understood by “objective” in the context of the neo-Kantian-inspired distinction of the economic “object” of experience and the “object” of knowledge?

The distinction is supposed to hold for natural no less than for social sciences; yet is it not the case that here, under “object” no less than under “reality”, something fundamentally different is thought each time? Is it not so that a quite other sense pertains to the objectivity of the social-scientific “object” of experience as of knowledge than pertains to that of the natural-scientific one? In Simmel's words: if the economic “object” cannot possibly be “objective in the same sense”, what consequences does that have for the distinction of “object of experience” and “object of knowledge”? Does it not become void altogether, if Amonn holds to his own thesis, that “reality”, and hence the “object” too, in the socio-economic sphere has a “quite other sense”? Is there not asserted hereby that very thing which, in general, deprives the distinction of object of experience and object of knowledge of its sense — namely, that the material given in advance by no means constitutes a chaotic, unstructured multiplicity, but one structured in itself, that these differences therefore are real ones? Could Amonn, then, concede that with money the problematics of universals pointed out by Simmel arises, so that even the “object of experience” is articulated, in itself and “objectively”, into universal and single? As a neo-Kantian he cannot do so on any account, because under this aspect too the principial difference of object of experience and object of knowledge would have become void.

On another aspect of the problem of money, the problem of the real relation of commodity and money, there likewise showed itself the entire groundlessness of the neo-Kantian formation of concepts. The matter is this: if it cannot at all be what matters, as regards Marx's and Simmel's “objective being-for-one-another” of things, and if rather, in Max Weber's sense, “not the *‘thingly’* connexions of *‘things’*, but the *intellectual* connexions of problems” are supposed to constitute the socio-economic “object of knowledge”, then it follows from this that even the “thingly connexion” of commodity and money on a global level, in short: of flows of goods/volume of trade and of money flows/quantity of money, that is, the equation of exchange, cannot come into consideration as a legitimate “object of knowledge”. Because Amonn will presumably have sensed this absurd consequence of neo-Kantian concept-formation — one may recall Adorno's critique of Weber —, he does on the one hand profess the methodological credo of Weber — after all, it underlies his dichotomization of the object —, yet on the other hand he develops theses diametrically opposed to it, doing justice to the thing in contradiction to his own method. Thus he defines the entrepreneur as the one who “transforms his power of disposal over (...) concrete objects of exchange back into an *abstract* power of disposal represented by money”.[^159]

This “transformation” — usually called “metamorphosis” — is of course a classical example of a “thingly connexion of things”, of a real relation: the commodity relates itself to money as that which is transformable, and money to it as a result of its transformation. The concrete does not here stand indifferently opposite the abstract, but exists only in relation to it, just as, conversely, the abstract exists only in its objective, real relation to the concrete. In crude contradiction to the neo-Kantian postulate, Amonn therefore arrives at some astonishing theses: “what matters in economics is a definitely qualified connection (!!) of things (...) in a definitely qualified social (!) connexion”; — “an *external relation of things to one another, independent of personal states*”; — “price (...) is an *objective* (!!) category and contains logically nothing subjective”; “that which is *logically earlier* and *primordial* is the *objective social phenomenon of value*”.[^160]

All these are highly apt findings, located right on the boundary of the subjective theory of value, and to be found, within the subjective mainstream of academic economics, at most also with Zwiedineck and Karl Elster. Marx could have taken these sentences without reservation into his chapter on fetishism and could have made use of them to ground an objective theory of value. To be sure, there is nowhere to be found in Amonn an attempt to make precise the concept of an “objective category”; namely, to determine the meaning of “objective” and to clarify the sense of “category” in relation to the concepts of “object of experience and object of knowledge”. And so it came about that Amonn was able at once to weaken again, indeed even to turn into its opposite, these provocative theses on the “*social* relation of things”,[^161] in the sense of an “*objective* category”. “Price” — that exceedingly complicated structure of which the theories of price of economics know nothing to report — “is not a merely external technical (?!) relation between goods (...), but, according to its proper essence, an *inner relation of the two exchanging individuals to the (...) object*. This inner relation is the truly social thing (...); the external relation of the (...) objects is only the *expression* of this inner relation.”[^162]

As one sees here, “price” turns out, to the academic economist, to be something highly mystical as soon as it is thematized as “form”; in Amonn nothing is to be learned as to how these theses stand toward his earlier ones. A similar confusion, and moreover something which immediately contradicts the dichotomization of the economic object, is to be found also in the following quotation, in which he thematizes the “form” of the commodity, that is, of the commodity-money relation: “‘Commodity’ is (...) an object of exchange insofar as it *represents capital*, i.e. (...) abstract individual power of disposal (...), *which means (!) that it can be transformed into the money-form*. Without relation to money the concept (?!) of the commodity is unthinkable. The two concepts (?!) money and commodity stand in a necessary logical relation, they are *correlative concepts*. An object of exchange is a ‘commodity’ if a (...) sum of money (...) can be substituted for it without changing the meaning (!!) of the object of exchange. The commodity is therefore, like money (...) a *particular form (...)* of abstract power of disposal, or of capital”.[^163]

In the first instance, “commodity” is spoken of in the sense of that “reality” of economics which “is not sensuously experienceable”, of the “commodity” as “representation” of a social “power” — “abstract” to be sure, yet still capable of being transformed into something concrete —, which as such cannot be a concept but must exist; in short: he makes precise what he had designated as the “*social* relation of things” and as an “*objective* category”, and what is in question is evidently their capacity for “transformation”, that is, for metamorphosis. But already in the next sentence the real relation becomes a necessary logical relation, and the “real” becomes something conceptual. They are supposed to be “concepts” which stand in a “necessary logical relation” — an assertion which runs counter to Simmel's thesis of a “relativity between the things” rather than between concepts, to the thesis of the “liveliness” of their real “interaction”; in the third sentence the subjective concept transforms itself, underhand, into an objective one, respectively the thing into an objective “meaning”, hence into a sensuous supersensible; in the fourth, finally, the reconversion of the concept into something real takes place, namely into a “particular form” which, as a *real* form of appearance per se of a real third, of capital, can scarcely still be misunderstood as a “concept”.

This permanent interchange of the logical and the real rests by no means on unlucky formulations; for Amonn's interpretation of another relationship, that of “price” and of the “ideal measure of prices”, contains the same transition from the logical level to the real one, in so far as he characterizes it now as a logical, then again as a real one: there is talk of a “mutual logical conditioning” such that the one “concept” (!) necessarily “arises and persists” with the other.[^164] It goes without saying that such an objective mode of being by no means accrues to subjective concepts; in fact the “ideal measure” is supposed to be more than a “concept”, for the task is accorded to it of effecting something objective: it “constitutes (!) (...) homogeneity”,[^165] and therewith socio-economic objectivity.

Beneath these contaminations of the logical and the real, in the context of the theory of money, there will lie above all two unsolved problems. In the first instance what is in question is the problem of a “third”, whose “reality” must be of another kind than the physical one; and evidently the peculiar structure of this “third” consists in this, that it is not separated from the “conceptual” by an unbridgeable gulf, because, as an existing universal, it is a logical-real. The economic real abstraction implies the necessity — upon which Adorno insists in particular — of “reckoning” the “conceptual” to reality, not however to reality as such, but to the “social” one. The analysis of the economic object in a theory of society consists precisely also in this, that one “reckons the conceptual to social reality. What is meant is (...) conceptuality (...) as something holding sway in the thing itself”[^166] — “a moment of subjectivity in the object”.[^167] With his permanent switching of the levels of the logical and the real Amonn supplies an indirect and involuntary confirmation of the doctrine of real abstraction, as Adorno has developed it in its methodological consequences. The other problem not mastered by Amonn, which underlies those strange contaminations, is the relationship, relevant in the context of the problem of universals and left by him wholly unclarified, of abstract and concrete, of abstract, objective and absolute value. On the basis of the pertinent literature on the problem of value, which can be touched on here only superficially, one may say without exaggeration: “abstract value”, brought “to appearance”, is the “secret” pure and simple of academic economics; it leads in it a ghostly existence such that it is to be found everywhere and nowhere; mostly condemned to appear under other names, it often leads only an existence in footnotes; thus for instance in a modern textbook of the theory of money such as that of R. Richter. In the main text there is talk of money which, as is known to the present-day theorist of money, splits into a “concrete” and an “abstract” money. As a “unit of account” it is supposed to be an “*abstract money*”; what is in question here, it is said, are “units of account bearing some currency name ((...) cigarettes, pounds of gold (...), dollars)”; and banished into the footnote there is then found a brief reference to the proper secret of economics, to be sure a concealing rather than an elucidating one: “The ‘ideal unit’, also designated in the literature as monetary unit (...) unit of value, cf. A. Nussbaum (1925, 6)”.[^168] What sort of *value*-unit is this, in what sense is the concept of “value” used here, if it can evidently not be a “subjective” one?

Now, in the fifties the authors on the theory of money were still so unabashed as to use, in this context too, that riddle-word which Richter apparently scarcely dares to take into his mouth, let alone to think: that the unit of value is an “abstract” one, so that the inference lay near, that the “measured”, “transported”, “stored value” etc. can scarcely be a subjective one either, but must likewise be an “abstract value”: as in the twenties — for example with Knut Wicksell[^169] — it was still a commonplace formulation that the “unit of account represents an abstract (...) magnitude of value”, that promises of payment run on “abstract units of value”, and that money represents a “supra-individually grounded representation of value”, hence not a subjective one; that it is capable of “storing (!!) an abstract sum of value” and is to be comprehended as the result of the “detachment of an abstract concept of value”.[^170] To be sure, according to so prominent an author as Albrecht Forstmann, money is supposed to measure, transport etc. not merely “objective values” but even “absolute (!!) values”[^171] — which, as Simmel and Schumpeter emphasize, can have their place only in the labour theory of value —, while in general the fundamental problem stays unclarified, of how the concepts, respectively the categories, of “abstract”, “objective” and “absolute” value are to be thought.[^172]

Richter rightly refers to an author of the twenties, for at that time certain efforts to master the problem of the abstract unit of value were to be found in the German literature; yet it is precisely those far better-known authors whose works offer just the material out of which the *antinomical* character of this field of problems can be worked out with ease, that he ignores.

The problem of “objective” and of “abstract value” is, however, not merely one of the theory of money. Already at the beginning of the century it became relevant above all in the context of the great debates of the theory of capital, and scarcely anything changed in this in the second half of the century either: like a shot from a pistol, “abstract value” turns up, for instance, in Otto Veit, in the context of problems of the theory of capital: “nominal capital” is “an abstract sum of value expressed in monetary units”, and capital lent out is “an abstract amount of value in the shape of money”.[^173] There is to be found not a trace of an indication of how Veit — an avowed champion of the subjective theory of value — squares the compatibility of a “subjective” with an “abstract value”.

The great debate on capital value at the beginning of the century is of particular interest because it gave Amonn the immediate impulse to reflect on a reality sui generis; and this in crude contradiction to his position of Rickert/Weber, according to which the “*thought* connexions of the problems”, the grasp of their “*formal* peculiarity”, at the same time constitute the “formal cognition (...) of the object”,[^174] of the object qua object of knowledge. Now, by contrast, he finds himself, unwittingly, dealing with “very real things”, namely with capital and money as “objects” of a peculiar kind, quite unknown to the natural sciences — objects with a peculiar “*meaning*” (!), an objective meaning which pertains to them in themselves (!!): their supra-individual validity and “*meaning* (...) in *social intercourse*”;[^175] once again what is in question is no longer at all an allegedly “a-logical” object of experience, since to it too there is accorded, unwittingly and in contradiction to all neo-Kantian resolve, a rational thematizable peculiar structure of social peculiarity.

In the dispute between various representatives of the subjective theory of value, Amonn defends those opponents of Böhm-Bawerk in the theory of capital who, according to his account, defined capital value as “an abstract (!) quantum of productive capacity”, which is supposed to “embody” itself “in concrete things (...)”, a “mass of value ‘embodied’ in material goods”; such economists would illegitimately “insert something third (...) between the abstract concept ‘capital’ and the concrete capital goods, and, for this third, the attributes of real existence (...) claim (...) Such a third does not exist.” Böhm-Bawerk put the question in dispute into the formula whether one may comprehend capital goods as concrete “embodiments *of an abstraction*”: hence as a *real abstraction*. What is in question here is no longer the problem of quantitative relations, no longer the object of an economics in the sense of a “quantitative science”,[^176] but rather qualitative problems, which must elude the analysis of a “modern” economics defining itself as a quantitative science; what is in question are problems which now turn out to be quasi-ontological questions and culminate in the disputed question “whether something exists (!!) or does not exist” — formulated ontologically: what is in question is that which “there is”. In this sense Böhm-Bawerk polemicized also against Schumpeter, who had apparently crossed over into the opposing camp, in that he too spoke of “homogeneous masses”, indeed even of “stocks in an abstract sense”, and had proceeded from the existence of an “abstract (!!) sum of value, or ‘sum of purchasing power’, detached from every real stock of goods”. All these concepts reminded Böhm-Bawerk “at every step of Marx”, which alone sufficed to warn urgently against such “dangerous doctrines”.[^177]

And in the context of this memorable dispute Amonn pleaded for a “thorough reinterpretation of the old empiricist concept of reality”; he sided with those who had after all asserted a “real third in the world of actuality”, and indeed “a *very real third*”. Of this “third” qua capital he now gives a remarkable description: “In its appearance (!!) it is no doubt bound (!!) to things of a material nature, but it is yet something fundamentally different therefrom; not real-sensuous (!), yet something separable and self-subsisting in thought from its material substratum.”[^178] What is in question is “a *very real third*” because it “brings forth effects in the *social* world of actuality” — put otherwise: the “social world” is a “third world” beyond the physical and the psychical. It represents, then, that “new order” of things which, according to Wieser, “stands in contradiction” to all that we “are accustomed to think about the things between heaven and earth.”

Amonn seeks to make the existence of that “*very real third*” — “reality (...) in a quite other sense than in the natural sciences” — plausible in this way, that he ranges it on a level with other appearances of the “social world”: “state (...) family (...) property etc.”[^179] too are supposed to count among those “very real things” which “can be neither (...) seen nor (...) heard”. Yet what Amonn forgets here is the essential point, that these institutions by no means, “in the sensuously perceptible world”, “objectify (...) themselves, attain objective expression” in the way capital value does: who would assert that state, family etc. constitute something so “bound to real quantities of goods (...) yet, for all that, (...) distinct from them”[^180] as abstract capital value does in relation to the concrete capital goods.

A precise summary of all that Amonn had vaguely and contradictorily before his mind as properties of that economic “reality” ungraspable by natural science, and hence a positive determination of that “third”, is sought in him in vain. Sombart's late methodological writing seems to take account of this, when its first chapters are provided with the provocative titles “The Indeterminacy of the ‘Object’” and “The Indeterminacy of the Mode of Cognition”. It begins with the provocative sentence: in “economics (...) everything which should be determinate is indeterminate: even the object with which it occupies itself. And this is surely a peculiarity which (...) it has in common only with philosophy: that it does not know where it is located on the globus intellectualis”.[^181]

It is the old song, the age-old lament of the economists[^182] — a century earlier, Bailey had struck it up. And in 1936 Amonn found himself moved to quote comparable complaints from Lionel Robbins' famous writing on method: “We all talk about the same things, but we have not yet come to agreement about *what it is we are talking about*.”[^183] Yet the author of a hefty book on the “object (...) of theoretical economics”, which contained an extensive presentation of the history of this dispute and at the same time a new, apparently indubitable determination of the object, could not and might not assent to the resumption of the debate, since it seemed, with his definitions, to have been definitively settled. Sombart's newly grounded doubts therefore had to call him onto the field.

To be sure: Amonn's polemic against what Sombart himself designated a “purely spiritualistic determination of the concept of the economic state of affairs as a purely inward psychical process”[^184] was as much in its right as was his attack on the latter's confession to the existence of an “objective spirit”; and he is in the right in general when he polemicizes against the subjectivist determinations of the object, against the one of Robbins still favoured in today's economic literature, according to which the economic is a “form of human behaviour”, namely a “performing of acts of choice”,[^185] and against that of Liefmann, according to which economics has “to do not with objects (!!) but with psychical considerations”;[^186] yet the main argument of his critique of Sombart's thesis of indeterminacy could not convince, because it wholly ignored his own reflections on the “very real third”: the object as object of knowledge of science is always a determinate one, “with determinate problems a determinate object is necessarily determined”, whereas the real object of experience is such that it “cannot be determined conceptually on account of the principially infinite multiplicity. Nor can experience itself give us any point of view for the determination; we have in it no orientation whatever”.[^187]

Twenty-two years later, when he could appeal to the fact that his dichotomization of the economic object had become “established” in economics and had “so to speak become common property”, he specifies the neo-Kantian view of the problem of the determination of the object thus: the science must indeed “‘have’ its ‘object’ beforehand, it must be there before it, be given to it; but that is its *object of experience* (*Erfahrungsgegenstand*), and for this there is no concept. (...) The *object of experience* cannot be defined at all, for it is not a logical category.”[^188]

So can he confidently deny the question whether there is “not something given in advance and objective” which “can serve us as a guiding line”. What there is, are suddenly nothing but “*problems* and *statements*”, which are ordered according to differing “points of view” of research, and this in such a way that “under different points of view (...) the same things present themselves quite differently” to scientific consideration.

The confounding consequences of this neo-Kantian idealism are not merely academic ones. He illustrates them with a view to the Marxian concept of capital: “Of course there is (...) no sense in asking: ‘what *is* “capital”?’ and in answering: ‘“capital” *is* value begetting surplus-value, or something else.’” There “is” now no more anything “which is, in and for itself, ‘capital’”; as with the once criticized Böhm-Bawerk, there is now no real “third” any more. Rather, one is to ask what one “in the treatment of various problems” in each case “understands” by capital, whereupon it will turn out that, according to the respective point of view of the problem, it presents itself as quite and wholly “*different things*”. In this way Amonn lights upon a most brilliant method of eliminating some allegedly merely apparently unbridgeable oppositions, such as that between Marxian and neoclassical economics. In the neo-Kantian ordered “science” of economics there is room enough, under ever different perspectives, for Marx as well as for Mises, and the unspeakable controversies dissolve into nothing but harmony.

In the case of the category of capital this looks as follows: one leaves to the neoclassical school the so-called “point of view of the theory of production”, while it must not be overlooked that there is also the “‘social’ point of view”, the Marxian one, which thematizes “social dependence”, the “*social relationship*”. If Marx “says” (!) that capital is “value begetting surplus-value”, then one need not take such speech literally and criticize it; for under the “social” “point of view” thematizing dependence, one has done justice to the legitimate concern of the Marxian theory of capital; “this comes to the same thing”. The Marxian one “is one of the several concepts of ‘capital’ current in our science, and like the others it has its good sense, it is a thoroughly purposeful (!!) concept for the problematics of the (...) *social relationships*.” Amonn fully approves of that “point of view” in which one thinks of “relations of social dependence, of the *social opposition* (!!) of ‘capital’ and ‘labour’”, however: “in the specifically economic problematics of production there is no opposition between capital and labour”[^189] — and not even under the “point of view” of the theory of distribution; the Marxian, or “social”, one is therefore “purposeful” solely on the field of social policy.

The “social opposition” may be bridgeable in a spirit of social partnership, the one of distribution policy, and above all the socio-economic one, scarcely. The apologetic consequences of the Weber/Amonn methodology — they ground the neoliberalism of the Adenauer/Erhard era no less than the neo-Kantianism of the Godesberg SPD — follow of necessity from the neo-Kantian premises: if the so-called “object of experience” presents itself as a chaotic, unstructured material, then it is in fact quite meaningless to assume real relations between the things, and hence a heightened meaninglessness even to assert something like real oppositions and contradictions in the sphere of production and of distribution.

But even in this late essay on method, Amonn's memory of his early problematics had not entirely died out; in strikingly vague wording there is still talk of an “economic” which “we do not experience sensuously” — but such a thing is no “object of experience”, rather one has “only mentally (zu)” to grasp it. It then cannot, however, be an “object of knowledge” either, for this was nothing other than the “object of experience” considered under a definite “point of view”, merely one of its “aspects” or “sides”. Evidently he still remembered, from afar, the existence of the “concrete embodiments of *abstraction*” and Böhm-Bawerk's misgiving whether such “something really exists or does not exist” — that “real third” which for Amonn at that time still “existed”, although it can “be neither seen nor heard”, “capital” in and for itself; he had, however, to let it rest with this brief remark, if he did not want to fall into open opposition to his “point of view” analysis.

This “real third” is a real one only as something *really* distinct over against the other two “actualities”, that is, over against what is physical and what is psychical, so that “actuality”, and hence the so-called “object of experience”, is in truth by no means of an a-logical and amorphous kind, but rather just so structured, articulated trialistically. Amonn may have gained the impression that he had not succeeded in determining socio-economic “reality” positively as well — Simmel's “quite other kind of objectivity”, which is supposed to be “not objective in the same sense” as the physical —, and not merely negatively, as “reality” in that “quite *other* sense”, as a *not*-natural-scientific one. Explicitly he renounced a positive definition all his life, whereby he turned his ontological need into a neo-Kantian virtue.

But in his confrontation with the subjectivist Robbins and the latter's follower Stonier over “*what it is (!) that we are talking about*”, whether “human behaviour” qua “performing of acts of choice” or something else, he could no longer confine himself to negative statements and appraisals. An analysis of “human behaviour” does not of itself figure for him as an “economic” one; he therefore characterizes it as mere “analysis of data”, which relates merely “to psychical, natural and technical facts”, and can accordingly content itself with the dualistic articulation of the “facts”, of the actual — the articulation into “psychical facts” on the one side, “natural and technical”, hence physical ones, on the other; the subject matter of an analysis which is economic in the proper sense, however, “is something *quite other*”, namely an “analysis of the relations of an exchange economy, of prices, wages, rents etc. — which yet are evidently *not* ‘human behaviour’ (!)”[^190] — but such as has to be regarded as “*phenomena of another kind*”. Amonn is thus compelled to ask after the mode of being of such “facts”; and he has to pose this ontological question because he wants to have them distinguished from action itself, as its “results”: “prices, wages, interest, rents etc. are (!) something quite other than ‘relations of actions’ (...) naked facts which are the result of quite definitely qualified (...) actions”.[^191]

What is asked after, then, is the otherness, or the being-other, of the “results” of action, which, as such, still belongs to the sphere of what is psychical; this being-other has to be understood as a becoming-independent over against those who act, hence also over against what is psychical, and it is to be borne in mind that this “real third”, now surfacing again, is not merely a “result” of action but at the same time its *presupposition*, which constitutes an essential reason why one has to ask after a mode of being of its own, namely a supra-individual one, over against the individual act of acting. In contrast to the reflection of this problematics in Gottl, Amonn has nowhere taken account of this dialectic of presupposition and result.

There were further reasons to insist on the difference of economic categories and of actions; in their own lawfulness they determine actions and possess the capacity for a “transformation” into one another, for the so-called “metamorphosis”. Not only Turgot, Say, Ricardo operated with this concept; Amonn himself had described a “transformation” of “concrete objects of exchange” into “*abstract*” ones; within the dualism of things physical and psychical which lies, unconsciously, at the basis of southwest-German neo-Kantianism, there was indeed no room for a “transformation” of something physical into something abstract, and vice versa; Amonn therefore refrained from pressing these arguments.

How, then, does it stand with the possibility of predicating positive determinations for the being-other of economic categories over against actions? One must be clear about this: that with the possibility of an answer to this question there is co-decided also whether it is methodologically legitimate to speak of “capitalism” as a “very real”, namely a lawfully-in-itself regulated, “third”, of “capitalism” in the sense of a “totality” of real categories, of a whole which is more than the sum of its parts. Even if Amonn was hopelessly overtaxed when it came to answering precisely the questions he had himself posed, one will nevertheless have to praise him for having posed them at all, all the more since the philosophy of his time left him in the lurch completely.

To be sure, he was not the only economist to have asked after a mode of being of the economic object in its own right. The acute Carl Menger had noted, with regard to social origin and presumably also to structure, “a most intimate kinship”[^192] between “money”, and the economic categories in general, on the one side, and “language” on the other — a hint which none of his adherents took up, and which played no role in the Methodenstreit any more than did his reflections on a new logic of part and whole: these have to be “mutually *cause* and *effect* at the same time”[^193] — Menger, then, intended in the matter a dialectical logic of the social organism —, a logic precisely of that which Marx had called an “organic system”, in which “each thing set is at the same time a presupposition” (42/203), a “peculiar logic” of that “peculiar object”, capitalism, whose existence as a “real third” must be denied by neo-Kantianism and, beyond it, by Kantianism as such.

Menger's comparison has a long tradition; it reaches back to Johann Georg Hamann, is to be found again with Karl Bühler, and was then taken up by Bruno Liebrucks: he comprehends language and money as “real-ideal” objects, which he also characterized as “trans-objective objects”, whereby he laid at the basis of his analysis above all the Hegelian doctrine of the “second supersensible world”, to which also Marx's basic concept of economics, that of the “sensuous supersensible thing”, owes its origin. Liebrucks set forth that the analysis of such “objects” requires the elaboration of a philosophy beyond the dualism of “consciousness” and “being”, a philosophy of “being-conscious”. With it one does, to be sure, move to a standpoint beyond the neo-Kantian methodology, which now presents itself as a classic object of demonstration of what, from the standpoint of critical theory, has to figure as a product of “narrowness of the understanding”.

Amonn was unequal to his task of distinguishing economic categories from actions in a quasi-ontological manner, that is, in that of a theory of society, above all for the reason that the neo-Kantian methodology, which he praised as the “newer logic”, blocked his view of the problems ably designated by Carl Menger — and this not only for him, but also for the German economics oriented to his and to Max Weber's methodology. All the more astonishing, then, when he, in contradiction to his methodological self-understanding, kept his view free, now and then, for the “very real third” — thus for the “real-ideal object”, or precisely for the “sensuous supersensible thing” —, and thus arrived at formulations which agree almost word for word with some of Marx's, above all with those from the *Grundrisse*: the theory of economics relates its statements “*not to human behaviour*, but to the behaviour (!!) of an exchange magnitude as object (...) what *is* (!) the (...) object? (...) Quite clearly, it is the *exchange magnitudes* themselves and their mutual relations, respectively their behaviour (!!) towards one another upon a change of the one or of the other”.[^194]

He thus does not pose the question which, from his understanding of method, alone is legitimate — what do we “understand”, under certain “points of view”, as object qua object of knowledge —, rather he asks, illegitimately, after its mode of being. Here, of course, the proper problems only begin, which had to prove themselves, for him and for the economics which followed him, ultimately unsolvable. Against the early Schumpeter he had objected that the latter was ignoring something decisive, for what is in question in theory too are “not pure relations of quantity (...) or purely mathematical relations of magnitudes, but relations between real, empirical magnitudes, and there one must (...) always ask oneself: *what* is expressed? and: (...) is the expression adequate to what is expressed, are all the conditions which matter in the object (!!) taken into account in the expression?”[^195]

Amonn therefore asks: what constitutes the reality of the magnitudes, of the “exchange magnitudes”, that is, of economic quantities? In what do the reality and the being-so of that which “*is* (...) expressed” consist? Of a something which cannot be comprehended as a psychical one. And of which “object” is there talk in all this? Of the amorphous “object of experience”? Evidently not; yet of the “object of knowledge” still much less, for how should it receive its “adequate expression” in “real magnitudes”? When Sombart, as also Wicksell, Budge and others, asserted that in economics everything turns on “the gaining (...) of computable (...) facts”, which however are “quality-less (!!) magnitudes”, indeed “pure quantities”,[^196] Amonn replied quite correctly that “it is only with such that one is dealing in mathematics (...) All magnitudes in physics and in the other real sciences mean quantity of a definite quality”.[^197]

But if, according to his own thesis, “no subjective moment”[^198] can come into consideration in the case of the “*objective exchange value*” — of a “*social phenomenon of value*” and of an “*objective*” phenomenon, of a “*social appearance of value given objectively outside the single individual*” —, thus no moment explicable by the subjective theory of value; if, then, what is in question is not to be psychical qualities, yet cannot be physical ones either, then there arise two questions unanswerable for Amonn and for academic economics.

For the subjectivist-oriented economics can no more answer the question “what is being expressed” than can Amonn himself, and the same holds for the neo-Ricardian one. For if it cannot be a psychical quality, while “pure quantities” as existing are unthinkable, then the economist can never at all say what that is which he computes day by day. It remains for him an eternally unknown something, a thing in itself. Amonn reproached Gottl with this: that “we never learn *for what*” the number is supposed to stand — a correct finding; yet Gottl himself knew of the problem, he wanted, to be sure, to look past it “for the time being” — past “*from what* this characteristic number actually offers the extension, the amount (...) the question of the unit and of the sense of these magnitudes”.[^199] From this “for the time being” there became, to be sure, a definitive one; and Gottl was to postpone all his life the answering of the question he had posed himself, after the “*from what*” of economic quantities, just as Amonn for his part let his question after the “for what” of units of currency fall, not merely unanswered but immediately.

If academic economics continues to repress the question of the “from what” and the “for what” of its quantities, then this is evidently because it is not able to articulate in a meaningful way the question of the mode of being, or of the mode of “being given”, of economic quantities. Although, for instance, Amonn unceasingly makes use of the attribute “objective”, there has never for a moment occurred to him, nor to any economist, the problem of Simmel, that the qualities sought can be “not objective in the same sense” as the physical ones. He, and the economics which followed him, therefore also omit the obvious question: in what sense does neo-Kantianism speak, and is able to speak, of the “*object* of experience”, more precisely, of the “object” of experience; perhaps this philosophy owes itself to an erroneous identification of “object as such” with the “physical” object, whereby it then uses this restricted concept of the object, unreflectedly, so comprehensively that social appearances, too, can apparently be subsumed under it without a problem. This possibility of a category mistake has never been raised by its adepts in economics; and thus the suspicion lies on the surface that we are dealing here with merely a special case of “positivistic” thinking in the sense of Adorno, thus with the confusion of first and second nature, whereby the further question forces itself upon one whether the philosophy of Windelband/Rickert has not unreflectedly proceeded from a dualistic ontology.

Be that as it may: in describing a self-active “behaviour” of “exchange-value magnitudes” qua real and reflexive relations — that is, the “objective being-for-one-another” of things in Marx and Simmel —, and in asserting a possibility of “transformation” of the concrete into the abstract within this third sphere of being, Amonn makes, unconsciously, positive statements about the structure of that “very real third” which he himself has always merely circumscribed negatively, because it is proscribed by his own methodology. And it occupies him under two further aspects as well.

The “real third” was in substance largely identical with Gottl's fourth, the “economic dimension” of things; but an analysis of its structure did not please Amonn at all. One of its properties in particular irritated the neo-Kantian Amonn beyond all measure: it is supposed to be “*at the same time* a norm *and* something factual”. What was in question was indeed something very strange to him: “that is a positively mystical essence”, whose existence rests upon Gottl having “mingled together a few concepts which are logically wholly disparate”. And yet he himself had established, with regard to the ideal unit living immediately in his “general”, or “social consciousness (...)”, thus with regard to the abstract unit of value, that there accrues to it a “validity, or — if one prefers — the ‘power in exchange’”.[^200] In so far as this “unit” now embodies itself in money, it is an existing unit, and “validity”, as a “force” which is, is an existing one. There appears, accordingly, to be present something like a real “mingling” of what is formally-logical disparate, a unity of being and of figuring (Sein und Gelten). In fact, “objective exchange value” is frequently defined, quite in the sense of Gottl, as an “objective validity of goods”, as for instance by Böhm-Bawerk.

Amonn will have been unaware of this — as presumably were Rickert and Max Weber too —, that what they would have reproached Gottl with as a “mingling” of “concepts which are logically wholly disparate”, thus the proscribed “unity of being and ought”, is what defines that which Hegel calls “objective spirit”: “a spirit which *exists* and *figures* (...), an existence which exists so long as it *figures*”. What is in question is that “ethical substance”, that something whose “meaning” was uttered in the sentence of Hegel: “*it is and it figures*”.[^201] It is this sentence, which negates the neo-Kantian dualism of being and of figuring, that one has to comprehend, and to make use of, as one of the keys to an understanding of the Marxian value-objectivity.

It is precisely this Hegel/Marxian “theorem” which would have offered Amonn too a key to the understanding of his “very real third”; but as a neo-Kantian such “minglings” had to be as offensive to him, indeed as “mystical”, as Gottl's definition of the fourth dimension is: “the ‘becoming-objective’ of the ‘interwovenness’ of exchange relations” — a merely different expression for Simmel's “relativity become substance” or for Marx's “value-substance”, which realises and sublates itself in the commodity-money relation.

Finally, the neo-Kantian Alfred Amonn puts, with regard to Gottl's “Economic Dimension”, and implicitly with regard to his own “very real third”, two further questions which mark him out as a characteristic representative of the “thinking of the understanding”, which may know only the disjunctive form of “either/or”. In the first instance he finds it strange that under this dimension one would “have to think something other than a mere representation, not merely something purely subjective, but something objective in some way”; at the basis of the question lies, then, the representation that something can only be a “purely subjective” one, or only the opposite of this, something “objective in some way (!!)” — tertium non datur. The existence of something subjective/objective in some way lies outside the power of representation of Amonn, and of the economics which followed him. Presumably there lay at the basis of this question, too, the suspicion of a yet again “mingling” of what is “logically” wholly “disparate”.

Amonn formulates the second question unambiguously disjunctively: “Is the ‘Economic Dimension’ merely a *semblance*, or is it something real?”, and he thinks he knows that it is something which there simply “is not, a phantom which has no reality.”[^202] What, then, “is there” in the socio-economic space? As an “object of knowledge”, or as a structured real object? Might there “be”, perhaps, something third — Marx's “objective semblance” (*gegenständlicher Schein*) — which, like his “sensuous supersensible thing” and his “social natural property”, likewise constitutes the result of a “mingling” of things “wholly and entirely” disparate? Already in his confrontation with Gottl, more comprehensively than in the debate with Robbins, there poses itself the fateful question of economics, after that which “there is” and how it “is given” — as something objective or as something subjective, as being or as semblance. If one adds the problem of universals and the problem of validity, we arrive at the finding that, in the centre of the allegedly autonomous particular science of economics, at the fundamental question after the existence and the being-so of economic quantities, at least four fundamental questions of philosophy open themselves up — with the thematization of the problem of the “indeterminacy” of the economic object, “thought does indeed step out of the framework of economics”.[^203] This comes to the light above all where Amonn transgresses the boundaries of his own methodology. He assures us that there is nothing which “adheres” to the things, rather:

“what there *is*, are *acts of exchange*, and *relations of exchange between (...) things* given through them (!?) (...) or, taken precisely, a *relation between the subjects of exchange* (in general, independently of the concrete acts of exchange)”. Of this mystical, allegedly merely “complicated social state of affairs”[^204] nothing is to be learned, here or later; yet one may surmise that he is referring herein to his “social” or “general consciousness”, an entity which must indeed also be counted among that which “there is”, which, to be sure, there can “be” only beyond the neo-Kantian dichotomy of “object” of experience and “object” of knowledge.

Mystical, however, is above all this: that an “immediately living ideal unit” which not merely exists in, but even lives in, the “social” or “general consciousness”, and which also appears as a “mental”, an “abstract” and an “objective” one, is supposed to enter into a “connection” with the “real” object, a connection which is also designated as an “embodiment”, a “chaining”, a “binding”, a “representation” and a “realization”. The “real”, the “external” or the “material” is mostly money, yet on one occasion also the commodity; and besides that “unit”, which frequently appears as an “objective or objectivized (!!) unit of value”, there is likewise to be found with him “*social relations*” which are to “link onto” objects and to constitute their “social ‘side’ or ‘form’”. Thus not only money, but even “price” itself is something exceedingly strange — for it comes about through the fact that a “sum (...) of abstract, mental (...) units of account acquires a visible, intuitable expression in a real or empirical (!) object — is, as it were, embodied in a real good.”

As to how one is to represent to oneself processes of this kind, by which an “objective or objectivized unit of value” is “chained to an external (...) material sign”, nothing is to be learned from Amonn. He merely assures us that what is in question here is a “group of very involved and complicated problems”,[^205] which evidently appeared to him so “involved” that, like Gottl, he was never again to concern himself with them, as regards the question of the “From what?” of economic quantities. In short: if Amonn criticizes Gottl for letting price, as a number, “*affix itself*” to an object, then he must have forgotten his own formulations, which turned out still more reified. Now, what is in question is by no means peculiarly Gottl's or Amonn's eccentricities of terminology. They occur in Amonn only so frequently because his comprehensive investigation turns to *object and basic concepts* of theoretical economics; with ease he could have quoted from the texts of the classics of this discipline, who make use of the same terminology.

One may recall Böhm-Bawerk's polemic against those theorists of capital who wanted to comprehend capital goods as “embodiments of an abstraction”, and who try, with “florid phrases, ever new words and images (...), to throw across the longed-for bridge from abstract to concrete”.[^206] Böhm-Bawerk has here described only the special case, within the theory of capital, of that fateful problem of the “embodiment” of an “abstraction” — of the “real abstraction” in the sense of the Marxian and Simmelian formation of concepts. In truth, the excellent formulation of the “bridge from abstract to concrete” describes the central problem of the formation of concepts in economics as a whole. In the literature on the theory of value and of money it comes most clearly to the surface. It is here that one learns strange things of those “ideal units”: of the “concretization of that abstract unit of value”, then, the concretization or realization of something abstract, a “concretisation” in the sense of a “becoming-matter of the unit”; indeed even that a “process of incarnation (...) is the peculiar act of the becoming of money, in which, as it were, the ‘spirit’ of money existing in the economic order sinks down into an institutional body”.[^207]

The talk of “embodiment” is, in the most recent literature too, by no means confined to the process of incarnation of the spirit of money; rather: “commodity values can (...) embody themselves in (...) stocks of goods.”[^208] What distinguishes such an “embodiment” from that mystical process in which the spirit of money “sinks into a body”? That the present-day theory of money has advanced not a step, over against the older literature, with regard to the “longed-for bridge from the abstract to the concrete”, while in categorical penetration it has fallen far back behind Simmel, is shown by Richter's remark that the “minimal regulations” of the monetary order evolve “in some way or other” through a “process of an invisible hand”. And since he also seems, with regard to what he calls “connection”, the “connection of the unit of account with units of quantity of a good”,[^209] to be referring to Schumpeter, it can be shown how the formalized theories have fallen back behind the problem-awareness of the older literature.

If Schumpeter could not bring himself to publish the manuscript which he wrote amid “sighs”, this will have to do, also and precisely, with those concepts which slip so easily from the lips of modern authors: the concepts of “embodiment” and of “connection”. The question posed by Heidegger too, how the “adhering” and “being-burdened”, the “sticking” of things with a value, is “to be grasped categorially”, may well have been one of those which elicited from him the “sighs”. For it will have been a problem which occupied him, one which Amonn described, more or less unconsciously, with regard to the relationship of capital value and capital goods. He remarked “that by capital is to be understood not a real quantity of goods, but something bound, indeed, to real goods, yet essentially distinct from them.”[^210]

The existence of a “bridge from the abstract to the concrete”, doubted by Böhm-Bawerk, is identical with the possibility of an “embodiment” of the so-called “abstract power of disposal”, which is “bound” to “things of a material nature” in such a way that it can nevertheless be, and remain, something “distinct” from them. With Schumpeter, too, what is in question is the “embodiment” of something abstract, the fact that the “monetary unit (...) must, in some way (!), be ‘embodied’”, “as, e.g., in (!) a piece of metal”; in this matter it “is hard” for him “to see through” the fact that such a “connection (...) evidently does not yet mean logical identity”.[^211] In this fundamental question of Schumpeter's, which Richter has probably not perceived at all and which has remained wholly unknown to the modern solvers of the “riddle of money”, the discussions conducted so far culminate — discussions of the “adhering” of prices, qua power in exchange, to economic things, thus to commodities, to money and to capitals, and of their “being stuck” with a value. Nowhere does their predicament of determining the object, and therewith that which Gottl called the “logical misery” and, more specifically, the “methodological tragedy”[^212] of economics, come to light more clearly — a tragedy which also manifests itself in this, that three quarters of a century later their “lack of a conception in the theory of science”[^213] once again had to be lamented, presumably once again without any result.

How should it succeed for economics, after all, to construct the synthesis, that is, the “bridge from the abstract to the concrete” which is untraversable from Böhm-Bawerk's point of view — that is, to think nothing other than the unity in the difference and the difference in the unity, this “objective” or “existing contradiction” — hic Rhodus, hic salta!

That academic economics will scarcely risk this leap, and that its object will therefore remain an “indeterminate object”, is a consequence of that style of thought which has been analysed, and traced back to its roots, more clearly and provocatively than by Marxist critics, by Bruno Liebrucks. He sees “Kant, and with him all the ‘philosophy of reflection’ so called by Hegel (...), hanging on a philosophy which will never know what money is.” And further: “Money, within whose spell all philosophy of reflection thinks, refutes the non-dialectical error that something cannot be, at the same time, a concrete single thing (*Sache*) and universal (100 DM).”[^214]

When Bruno Liebrucks remarks that, in the face of “phenomena such as (!) life and consciousness”, the principle of the contradiction to be avoided is only “valid in a sublated way” — that it itself “figures and does not figure”, since otherwise dialectics would always already have subjected itself to the principle of the excluded third —, then he was thinking, too, of the phenomenon of money. Thinking which is oriented to the philosophy of reflection, stuck in the oppositions of subject/object, particular/universal, being/validity, being/semblance and existence/concept etc. pp. — thus precisely also the thinking of economics — can therefore be determined, too, as a thinking which is not able to think money as an object sui generis, because “it divines nothing of its objectivity (!!)”[^215] — nothing of that “objectivity” which is not that of physical qualities.

This problem of the “objectivity” of that “real third” of Böhm-Bawerk/Amonn can scarcely be separated from the problem, thematized in the more recent discussion of method, of the relation of model and “reality”. If, then, everything must depend, for the Walrasian, on building “a series of ‘bridges’ from theory to reality”,[^216] and if, as one of these bridges, for the purpose of transforming the so-called “relative prices” into “absolute” ones, there is named the quantity theory and therewith the equation of exchange, then it is to be pointed out that this “bridge”, too, is a most brittle one — namely, that its macroeconomic entities must lay claim to precisely that “objectivity” which the economist, proceeding from his “understanding” comprehension of reality, is not at all able to apprehend conceptually. That this equation implies those values which, for the academic economist, may not exist at all, namely “abstract”, “absolute” and “objective” ones — and how it does so, all this can be read up on in Simmel and his critics. If one pursues this problematique of value, so blended with the equation, then every theory of equilibrium, however finely wrought in formalist terms, and every neo-Ricardian theory of prices, proves itself a gigantic petitio principii, insofar as it lays claim to that “objectivity” which either contravenes its subjectivist implications or else remains underived. The two “longed-for bridges” prove to be, in the ground, one and the same; for “reality”, so-called, does not, according to Amonn's demonstrations, consist merely of acts of choosing, but consists above all in that “objectivity” which Marx has characterized as an “abstract” one. The “logical misery” of economics has been described not only by Gottl, but also by other conservative critics of their discipline.

Thus Michael Kröll, who based his reproach, that economics is an “unbalanced, formless, diffuse” discipline, on the fact that it is not able to solve the “riddle” of the whole of the national economy — the ancient problem of unity, or of the “invisible hand”. This unsolved problem expresses itself, too, in the “lack of a basic concept”, on account of which economics resembles “a tree without a taproot”, which “any storm can lay low”.[^217] The late Schumpeter addressed the same dilemma of a missing basic concept: he enumerated cases “of the failure of the unifying force of the principle of marginal utility”,[^218] whereby he implicitly gives utterance to what must be at stake in the formation of economic concepts, and in contrast alike to the neo-Kantian and, in general, to the “positivist” methodology: namely, the winning of a “unifying principle” from which the structures of the whole of economics can be developed. It goes without saying that such a “principle” is able to fulfil its system-grounding function only under the precondition that a “real significance” or validity can be accorded to it, in something like the sense intended by Horkheimer — a validity which, to be sure, it can lay claim to only within the context of the “overall presentation of a determinate object”.[^219] “Only as a moment of the theoretical whole”, which is to result from a dialectical “presentation”, do the concepts here possess “real validity”.[^220] Briefly and to the point, Adorno says that “the concept must, to that extent, be the sufficient ground of the thing (*Sache*)”.[^221]

Friedrich Jonas insists that social economics must ask after the “ground of happening”, after the inner unity and autonomous lawfulness of the dynamic whole. But since this ground “is to be verified not by intuition but only in the concept”,[^222] while such an objective, “unconditioned concept” remains denied to us, the economist cannot possibly know whether, in his models, by means of which he “fictionally creates for techniques an object”, he is dealing with semblance-objects or with real ones. But belonging to knowledge is “that one knows of what one knows something”, and “that of what (...) knowledge is in fact knowledge”.[^223] Because economics does not know this, nor can know it at all, it produces no real knowledge, but only the “semblance of a knowledge”,[^224] and its reflection, that is, the question, formulated in a Kantian manner and answered in the Kantian spirit, of whether and “how economic theory as science is possible”,[^225] issues in scepticism.
